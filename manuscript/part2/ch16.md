# A Time and a Place for Everything

Barnum's eleventh rule sounds like a housekeeping tip and is
actually an economic argument. The person who does business by
rule, having a time and a place for everything, doing the work
promptly, will accomplish twice as much and with half the trouble
of the person muddling through, he says. And the trouble figure is
the one worth pausing on, because the chaotic workday is not
merely less productive; it is more expensive in the only currency
you cannot borrow. The half-finished piece gets started twice. The
letter opened four times costs four openings. The day that never
had a shape never ends, which is why the slipshod worker projects
the feeling of working without cease and still cannot point at
what closed.

## Loose Ends, and Where They Cost

The anatomy of the unsystematic day is repetition without
completion. He half-does one thing, is called away, half-does the
next, and at day's end his business lies at loose ends, and he
will never know when his work is done, because it never will be.
Every loose end keeps a small clerk inside it, reminding, nagging,
rehearsing what remains, so the unsystematic day taxes you twice:
in the redoing and in the carrying. The systematic day, doing one
thing at a time and meeting appointments punctually, finds
leisure for pastime and recreation at the end, exactly because it
spent its attention once. Barnum's promise is precise and testable
this week: system is not what takes your evenings. Disorder is
what takes your evenings, and charges you for the privilege.

Punctuality deserves its line in this argument, because it is
where the system touches other people and they can feel it. The
prompt person is not merely polite; they are running a shared
schedule that other people can plan against, and they collect a
quiet reputation for solidity that no advertisement can buy. The
person chronically twenty minutes late is not managing time badly
in the abstract; they are spending other people's planned hours
to fund their own disorder, at a rate the borrowed-from party
eventually notices. Barnum's rule holds in both directions of the
clock: have a time for it, and then be there when the time
arrives.

The caution he attaches is equally honest, because system has a
failure of excess. There are people, he says, who put things away
so carefully they can never find them again; there are offices so
formalized that the form outran the work, all theory and no
result, red tape with a coat of arms. Rule, speed, punctuality
exist for the sake of the work, and when the rule begins to cost
more than the disorder it replaced, the rule has become gossip
about the work. The test is always the same: does the system
close loops, or admire itself? A habit that takes a minute to
maintain and saves fifteen is a system. A habit that takes
fifteen to maintain and saves a minute is a hobby.

## The Hotel That Was Too Systematic

His story, drawn from a famous hotel of his day, deserves the
full telling because it is funny in the specific way that carries
teaching. The house was the best in the country, praised for and
proud of the rigid system pervading every department. At midnight
the proprietor would touch a bell for the delight of the guests,
and within two minutes sixty servants would present themselves
in the hall, one with a bucket, an exhibit of order as
entertainment. But the system was a living thing with opinions of
its own. One evening, a waiter dropped out just before service,
and the landlord, rather than seat the dining room fifty short,
pressed the boot-boy into a white apron with three minutes of
instruction: stand behind those two chairs, soup first, then ask
what they will have next. The boot-boy, a game soul, assured him
he understood the virtues of the system perfectly. Presently a
guest at his two chairs, impatient with the procession of
courses, asked for the bill before the plates were cleared, and
the new waiter, holding to his briefing like a rock in a channel
where he had never sailed, explained in ringing tones that here
in this house everything was done systematically: soup first,
and the bill afterward when the landlord's system said so. The
room got the bill from a senior hand; the guests got the story
for nothing; and the landlord got, at his own expense, the
cheapest audit a system ever receives. The system had not failed.
The system had never included a procedure for the procedure
breaking, which is the difference between systematic and
brittle.

Two readings attach to that dinner service, and this chapter
wants both. The first is for the household and small business:
build your system with a fault line in view. What happens when
the waiter drops out, the child wakes sick, the delivery slips a
day? A system with no answer to its own bad day simply reverts to
disorder at the worst available moment, and one meal in a hotel
is a small tuition bill for that lesson. The second reading is
for anyone tempted to build the show bell at home: part of what
the sixty buckets served was spectacle, guests being impressed,
the evening version of an office's beautiful chart. System is a
means; arrange to be twice as productive at half the trouble,
and do not draft rules whose main output is being watched obeying
them.

## The News Habit, Renovated

The other half of Barnum's pairing is the daily input that a
systematic week requires: read a trustworthy newspaper, and so
keep posted on the transactions of the world. His logic was that
the person without one is cut off from their species; his era
moved at telegraph speed, and the tradesman who stopped reading
found himself left out in the cold while inventions and
improvements rearranged his branch of trade around him. The
packet changed and he did not know the rate had.

Add the half of the argument he left implicit, because we can see
its edge better at this distance: early warning is a form of
compound interest. The tradesman who learns in March that the
bridge repairs next spring will cut his street's foot traffic has
time to renegotiate a lease, move a window, or book the summer
tourism he will need; the one who learns in March of next spring
is reading his own obituary in advance and may as well enjoy the
writing. Every market pays the informed small edges repeatedly:
the rate that was about to change, the supplier about to close,
the rule that takes effect in the autumn, the buyer whose
industry just announced a consolidation. None of these
transactions are secret. They are simply published in the
places systematic people read on schedule and other people
discover at their own cost.

Today the newspaper is a category, not an object, and the choice
is abundance, which changed the failure mode. His warning was
about insufficient input. Yours is about weightless, unverified
input, and the two sentences of the rule survive the swap
intact: one trustworthy source per topic that matters to your
business, taken on a schedule. The rates for your supplies, the
rule changes in your trade, the movement of your buyers, the
town's plans; these are the transactions of your world, and the
systematic worker reads them the way the pilot read the channel,
briefly and daily, before steering.
