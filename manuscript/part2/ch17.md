# Side Bets Sink Ships

The hardest chapters in Barnum's book are the ones he lived, and
these two rules sit next to each other on the page because they
ruined him together. A man of means, established in a business he
understands and has made prosperous, begins to hear the siren
voices: he is born lucky, everything he touches turns to gold, and
a grand speculation just outside his trade is prepared to prove
it. He puts in his twenty thousand. A few days pass and the deal
needs ten more to secure the position. Soon it is all right, but
unforeseen matters require a further advance, which will bring a
rich harvest. Before the harvest time arrives the bubble bursts,
the whole position is lost, and he learns at the price of
everything he had earned what he could have learned for free: a
man who leaves the business he understands for one he does not is
like Samson shorn of his locks; his strength has departed and he
becomes like other men.

His walkthrough also contains, quietly, the psychology of why the
losses never teach. The speculator, having lost, does not retire;
the excitement has him now, and he notices that others are making
large sums this way, and, as Barnum puts it, he looks for his
money where he loses it. Nobody in the cards room describes their
worst year; the winners narrate, the losers volunteer again. A
person can spend a decade inside impressions gathered from
exactly that sample, and this is the machine's second engine: the
outside market where you have no experience is also the market
where you have no calibration, no denominator, no honest friend
telling you the plain number. The legitimate business, for all
its daily grind, has the one advantage that matters when
everything else is uncertain: you personally watch the money
arrive, spend, and return, and the watching is the education
every bet forgets it needs.

## Why Competence Never Transfers

The mechanism is worth more than the moral, because the victim of
the mechanism is always a competent person. Success in your own
business rests on three assumptions you have earned and stop
noticing: you know what your customers are actually paying for,
you can read the day-to-day signals of your trade, and you feel
the size of money there because losing some has a taste you
remember. Step outside your trade and all three assumptions
evaporate at once, while your confidence, your optimism, and your
visible prosperity stay in the room. Barnum's modern
translation is blunt: nobody in a strange market knows whether
you have been told the truth about a "sure thing," and a person
flattered as lucky is the easiest signature to collect in
commerce.

His exception is on the record and worth keeping: a person with
plenty of money may, and perhaps should, put something into any
venture that appears promising and might benefit others. But the
sums moderate, sized as gifts you could watch vanish without
altering your position, and never the fortune earned in the
legitimate business. A side bet you can afford is entertainment
and tuition. A side bet you cannot afford is a poison dressed as
an acceleration.

## The Endorsement Machine

Then comes the rule he wrote in capital letters of pain: do not
indorse without security. His scenario deserves its walk-through,
because it is a machine that runs on affection and runs
beautifully. A friend worth twenty thousand, owing nothing,
proposes a quick profit and asks you to endorse his note for five.
You like him. He seems sound. You lend the name without paperwork.
The note comes back canceled; the deal worked; the feeling is
wonderful. The same thing occurs again, and again, and now the
impression is fixed in your mind: endorsing his paper is safe.
Meanwhile the friend has learned something you have not noticed.
He has learned to obtain money without effort. His own bank views
him through your signature, so every speculation of his, prudent
or silly, passes over your desk as a routine favor. Then one year
a deal goes long and a second note must be discounted to cover
the first, and then the speculation fails altogether, and your
friend does not tell you, because there is nothing to tell you
with. He tries again. The endorsing has become chronic on your
side, and at every loss he returns for your signature. Finally
both fortunes are gone, and the astonished ruin says, my friend
has ruined me; to which the clear-eyed version of you replies,
and I have also ruined him, because the tether was mine to set.

The pattern has outlived the paper form entirely, which is why
the walkthrough still matters. Today the same machine runs on
guarantor signatures on a lease, the card added to the family
plan, the LLC whose registered agent is you because it was
easier in April, the co-signed equipment loan for the cousin's
shop, the personal guarantee you gave the contractor because the
bank wanted one and he had a church connection. The modern
endorsement is quieter than Barnum's note: no desk, no canceled
paper, no moment of decision taken slowly. It arrives as a form
with a field your name goes in, frequently at a moment when the
surrounding warmth makes reading the whole document feel like a
suspicion. The rule transmits without amendment: your name is a
loan of your fortune's balance sheet, and every lender knows
exactly whom to collect from; make sure you know too, before
ink.

His remedy is one sentence and it preserves the friendship,
which is the remarkable part: I will accommodate you, but I never
indorse without taking ample security. Said in the first
conversation, it defines the relationship's limits where
definition costs a slight awkwardness. Held back until after two
years of free paper, the same sentence sounds like an accusation
and breaks the friendship instead. The threat endorses posture
that your name should be as hard to get as a bank's, and your
bank requires collateral, condition, and a face-to-face, and it
has your interest at heart in every one of the three.

## The First Dollars by Hard Knocks

The two rules point at one shared fact, and Barnum writes it as
advice to the young: people who get money too easily cannot
usually succeed. It is not a punishment; it is sensory. The
first dollars earned by hard knocks come with the taste of money,
which is the thing that later refuses to sign for a scheme it
cannot price; and the feel of money is what makes the siren
voices sound like voices rather than like fortune. Neither
rule says be ungenerous. They say be load-rated: give what you
can lose to what you can see, lend what you can price to whom
you can audit, and keep the part of your fortune that keeps you
comprehensible to yourself.

The family version of the rule deserves its own word, since the
requests most likely to arrive are from people you love. Barnum's
setting-down of the young starter was not a refusal; it was a
sequence. Let him understand the value of money by earning it
first, he says, and then, when the understanding is visible,
grease the wheels a little in helping him start. Help arrives
after the earned part, not instead of it, and the help that comes
too easily quietly disagrees with the whole education the young
person needs. That is not harshness; it is the same load-rating
decision as any other endorsement, with better weather and higher
stakes, because the borrower is someone whose education you are
personally invested in.

> **Do this now:** Write down every commitment of your money or
> your name that sits outside the business you actually
> understand: the standing endorsement, the stake in someone's
> venture, the deposit you have been carrying for a friend's
> deal. Next to each, write the amount you could truly spare,
> tonight, without changing your position. Any commitment larger
> than its spare figure gets untangled this month.
>
> **Track it:** All week, log every request you receive for your
> money or your signature: who asked, for what, and the answer
> you gave with its reason. Ten entries will show you what your
> name is currently used for by the people nearest you, and
> whether that use is the one you would have chosen.
