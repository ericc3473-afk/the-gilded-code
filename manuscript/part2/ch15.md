# Hope Much, Bet Little

There is a poor person on every street who is not poor from
idleness. They work constantly, in fact; they are always at the
center of something new, always plausibly about to succeed. Barnum
names the mechanism precisely: these people are kept poor by being
too visionary. Every project, examined exclusively from the
front, looks like certain success. And because each fresh certainty
arrives with the enthusiasm of a discovery, they keep changing
from one business to another, always in hot water, always under
the harrow. Betting households never starve from lack of bets;
they starve from the denominator, because every bet costs the
same thing, the season you withdraw from the one that was
starting to compound.

The old proverb about counting chickens before they are hatched
has not improved with age, he says, and adding machinery to the
counting has made it no more accurate. The modern version counts
with dashboards, projections, and the imaginary month when
everything happens at once.

So this chapter has two jobs, and they look contradictory until
they are held together. First, it has to protect your hope,
because the person who reads too many cautionary chapters stops
starting anything and becomes a different kind of poor: the kind
that never loses money because nothing was ever at stake. Second,
it has to take away hope's sign-off authority on the Portfolio of
Bets, because optimism is a terrible accountant. Keep both jobs in
view. Every paragraph below is one of the two hands.

## What Hope Is For, and What It Is Not For

The title of Barnum's chapter is doing double duty, and both
halves matter. Hope should predominate: he says so. A person
ruled by expectation of failure never starts, underbids, quits
early, and treats every setback as a verdict; the hopeful mind is
the only kind that survives the actual texture of building
anything, and half of this book's first part exists to install
that hope properly. But hope cannot be the underwriter. It is
fuel, and fuel is not a map. A bet sized by the size of the hope
is sized by the loudest gauge in the building; a bet sized by
evidence is sized by what the instrument says. Hope predominates
in the person. Evidence governs the bets. The two are only
enemies in people who have never separated them.

The visionary-changer humor always hides a misunderstanding of
what certainty is. A project cannot look like certain success,
because projects contain opponents: buyers who may not buy,
competitors who were already competent, seasons, rates, and your
own tired Thursdays. A projection that shows none of these has
not found a great opportunity; it has found a mirror. The
practical tell is easy to check. Ask the proposer of any
certainty what the second-best explanation of the early results
is. The person with a bet has an answer, usually an uncomfortable
one. The person with an inspiration has a complaint about the
question.

## Hammer One Nail

Then Barnum turns from the bettor to the builder and gives the
rule that hope without discipline needs to hear: engage in one
kind of business only, and stick to it faithfully until you
succeed, or until your experience shows you should abandon it. A
constant hammering on one nail will generally drive it home at
last, so that it can be clinched. The physics behind the picture
is attention arithmetic. A mind holding one object keeps
suggesting improvements of real value to it, noticed on the walk
home, at supper, on the drive back from the supplier. A mind
holding a dozen objects cannot perform the service for any of
them, because each subject's insights expire before the mind
visits them again. Fortunes do not slip away mostly through
losses; they slip through fingers busy with too many
occupations, every one of them individually defensible, and
their sum, dispersal.

This is the season-hour law, and it connects straight back to
chapter 4: ten percent of your waking attention builds the
picture, which means the other ninety percent cannot be spent on
four separate enterprises without the picture starving. People
misread the one-nail rule as anti-diversification for the
already rich, and it is nothing of the kind. Once a fortune
exists, spreading it is prudence. Until it exists, the
undivided season is the only instrument that drives nails. The
rule also permits its own exit, honestly stated: you stay until
success or until experience shows you should abandon it, and
experience is allowed to speak. It just must speak in data from
a whole season, not in Tuesday's mood.

## The imagined Calendar

One habit feeds the fire of both failures, scattering and
early certainty, and it is worth catching at the source: the
imagined calendar. Every enthusiastic new plan brings its own
imaginary version of your week, in which you have spare
evenings, unclaimed weekends, and a supply of fresh energy that
does not exist in the actual calendar. The imaginary calendar is
where the fourth project would live if you adopted it, and it is
roomy, because it is fictional. The real calendar, opened for
one minute, shows the truth: this week contains five open
evenings in total, and the first three projects have already
voted on them. Running new opportunities through the real
calendar before saying yes is the cheapest discipline in this
book, and the yes rate it produces is exactly the size of a
whole-self bet.

Neither failure form is cured by cynicism, which would be the
wrong universal solvent. The cure is the pairing this chapter is
named for. Hope like a person who expects the good season, and
spend like one who has read the weather; hold the single nail of
your season in the shop, and keep the shop sketches in a folder
labeled someday, where they lose nothing by waiting and gain
information every quarter you do not open them.

## The Cut List, and How to Feel About It

Since the instruction lands hardest on the assembler of projects,
say plainly what cutting is and is not. You are not failing the
discarded items; you are scheduling them into their only survivable
form. A project run at a fifth of the attention it needs is not a
project, it is a donation of money wearing a plan, and the someday
folder preserves the idea without taxing the season. Most people's
someday list, reviewed quarterly, makes the cut for them: half the
entries dissolve on contact with time, the ones worth keeping stay
kept, and the one that keeps returning with stronger evidence is the
next season's candidate, tested properly when the current nail is
clinchable. Barnum changed fields famously across his life, from
museums to tours to the circus. The changes look like scattering
and were the reverse. Each move was made once the current
enterprise was solid, funded the next one, and got his undivided
attention during its own passage. Serial focus, one at a time,
looks exactly like what the crowd calls opportunity. What the
crowd calls having your fingers in everything is the other thing,
and it is the poor man's version.

One last practical translation, for the employed majority who
have one job and no business portfolio: the one-nail rule still
applies to you, and its object is not your employer. Your working
season, the ninety percent, belongs to the trade from chapter 9;
your ten percent belongs to the asset from chapter 4. That is two
nails, and the rule holds. What scatters an ordinary life is
neither of those. It is the eight additional commitments, the
voluntary committees, the parallel schemes, the hobby that
became a podcast that became a merch line, each absorbing one
more night. When the family schedule reads like a courier
service, one nail is overloaded, and the overload is always
counted as virtue until the household surveys its year and
cannot name one thing that was driven home.

> working life. Cross out all but the one that most deserves the
