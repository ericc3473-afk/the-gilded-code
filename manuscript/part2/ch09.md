# Do Not Mistake Your Vocation

Barnum opens his money book with a rule, and he guards it with an
uncommon amount of tenderness for a showman: the surest plan for a
young person starting out is to select a vocation suited to their
taste and nature. His examples have aged well. Parents pushing a
bookish son into the family shop "until something opens"; a guided
daughter whose mother never once asked what the child saw when she
read. That kind of mismatch was common in 1880, and it remains the
quiet engine of the modern flat decade: competent people, adequately
paid, strangely tired, and no longer able to remember why they
started.

## The Two Bad Originals

Barnum names two failure shapes, and both still walk around. The
first is the trade chosen by somebody else's ambition: the son who
wanted the sea gets the insurance agency, the daughter who loved
engines gets steered toward "something steady," and both proceed to
do the work grudgingly and badly, becoming over the years exactly
what they were told they were. The adult version runs the same
mechanism with adult wages: the friend's approving nod, the alumni
newsletter, the voice that says the training was expensive so the
career owns you now. Expensive to leave, in other words, is not the
same argument as pleasant to occupy, and the two get confused for a
decade at a time.

The second shape is the person who never asks the question at all.
They fall into work by accident of birth and bus route, stay on
inertia through several careers' worth of years, and discover only
after they cannot stop being exhausted what one question would have
told them at nineteen: that they hate this, and are decently good at
something else. Barnum's phrasing is bracing. The person persisting
in a vocation nature did not intend them for, he says, is like a
fish trying to live in a tree, riding with every odd against it. You
can promote the fish, train the fish, decorate the tank; the fish
still is not eating.

## Word, Briefly, to the Parents

Barnum spends his opening paragraphs on parents and guardians, and
the address earns its space because it is the original source of the
problem. His picture of the household planning committee is gentle
sarcasm: the father with five sons who assigns one to the church, one
to law, one to the shop, with the confidence of a man dealing cards
rather than the humility of a man meeting a person. The modern
committee is the same shape with better manners: the degree selected
for its safety, the after-school hours filled according to the
neighbors' children, the trade recommended because the family
already owns the tools. None of it is malicious. All of it is
expensive, because a vocation installed by committee has to be
maintained against the resident's own preference, and maintenance
bills compound too.

So for every reader with a young person in their orbit: hand over
tools, not scripts. Exposure instead of assignment: the cousin's
kitchen at 6 a.m., the workshop, the accounting ledger in a real
small business, the stage crew. Watch what they return to when
nobody is watching, and fund that, said out loud, "this is not
obligation, this is interest." The whole family-wealth repeat cycle
runs on this detail, because children inherit either appetite approved
or appetite suppressed, and the second version is twice the work later.

## How to Check Your Fit

Taste is not well reported by mood on a Monday morning, so check it
the way an auditor would: from behavior, over weeks. Four
observations do more than any career questionnaire. First, at
closing time, notice which tasks from the day would still be
welcome: the hour disappeared, the interruptions irritated because
they broke something you were absorbed in, and you would take more
of it by choice. Second, watch what you do without being asked: the
shop you reorganize on a Saturday, the spreadsheet you redesign that
nobody assigned, the neighbor's problem you carried home. Voluntary
participation is the honest tell of a vocation, because it reveals
rather than promises. Third, notice whom you envy, and envy closely:
not the outcomes, the Tuesdays. A person whose ordinary workday
sounds beautiful to you has already told you where your taste is.
That is useful information whatever your friends earn. Fourth, and
cheapest of all, watch a free Saturday. The unpaid morning tells you
where your attention goes when no invoice is waiting: the stranger's
market stall is studied while the assigned pages nap in the bag. A
vocation leaves fingerprints on your spare time, and they are easier
to read than any list of aptitudes, because they were produced
without an audience and without permission.

Here Barnum lays in the honest pivot that the poster version of this
chapter always omits: taste can be trained. He points out that
people notch into a trade "not to their taste" and grow to like it
far past their expectations, because competence is delicious and
because the more interesting layers of any work only show their
soul to someone who has practiced it. His own biography is the
loaded example; nothing in his early ventures could have predicted
the museum, and no early verdict on his taste would have predicted
the showman either. So the law is not "follow your passion into the
sunset." It is: choose within what your nature can bear, then give
the judgment a term of trial before revising it. Two months of
actual effort is a fair trial. Six years of throttled exhaustion is
not a verdict on taste; it is a verdict on the schedule.

## The Version for People Who Are Already Employed

Employed readers will ask, fairly, what the law looks like on
Tuesday. Start by separating vocation from placement. If the work
itself is right and sits in the wrong conditions (bad management,
wrong city, wrong boss), your vocation is not the problem; law 10
handles the move within the trade. If the work itself is wrong, the
law gives one directive and no bluster: begin moving now, not after
the outside world agrees with you. Keep the job that pays while the
retraining pays nothing, learn something useful in the direction of
the trade you would actually stand (law 14 carries the instruction),
and let the move take a year if it must. What this law forbids is
only the permanent pretense that taste never enters the accounting.
It does, and it compounds either way.

A last word on the money side of the ledger, because taste-talk
sounds unbusiness. The right-vocation person collects two raises
every year: the stated one, and the one paid in appetite, which
shows up as faster learning, more hours that feel like hours, work
done at closing time on your own account. Across a decade of
earning, the compounding of that second raise is considerable, and
no spreadsheet accounts for it correctly. Barnum's severe arithmetic
says nothing different, only in shopkeeper's terms: a man who has
chosen work he dislikes must be paid extra to bring his attention,
and the market only pays that premium somewhere else.

> **Do this now:** Answer one question in your log, honestly: what
> work would I still be doing at closing time if nobody could see me
> doing it? Under the answer, write the version that could pay
> something this month, even one first dollar at the edge of the
> trade.
>
> **Track it:** For two weeks, log each task in your day as draining
> (D) or energizing (E), and name the task. The pattern between the
> columns is your vocation reading. Two full weeks before any
> totals: a bad Monday is not a verdict.
