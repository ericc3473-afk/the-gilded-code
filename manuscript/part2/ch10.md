# Put Yourself Where the Demand Is

Barnum carries directly on from the vocation chapter with a second
sieve, and it is the one most people skip: after choosing the work
your nature fits, choose the ground where that work is wanted. His
example is a hotel man. Run the finest dining room, manage five
hundred guests a day with clockwork competence, and you have proven
exactly one thing, that you can run a hotel; if the town holds
nobody who travels and no reason why anyone should, competence
starves politely, at a table set for excellence. The same person,
moved to a busy crossroads, eats well in his first season. Barnum's
point was never that talent varies; it was that demand has a
location, and merit only pays when it stands where somebody can pay
for it.

## The Many Shapes of Town

A century later the "town" has outposts, and it is worth naming
them, because the mistake now wears better disguises. A wrong
location is an industry in contraction, joined once by a parent and
maintained ever since by memory. It is the online shop stocked well
but parked on a platform where its buyers never walk, while the
competent rival sells the same goods where the foot traffic actually
is. It is the freelancer whose only referral channel is a cousin,
when the trade's real work is arranged in two professional rooms she
has been meaning to join since autumn. It is the manager with a
bulletin case of praise, employed by a company whose growth finished
in the last decade. In each case the professional is genuinely good.
The ground is genuinely empty. Barnum's hotel keeper is all of them
in a coat.

Platforms deserve their own warning, because digital geography
misleads people in the opposite direction from the obvious one. The
mistake is rarely "no internet presence" anymore; it is presence on
the wrong ground, which does the work of absence with extra
bookkeeping. A platform where your trade's buyers linger is a street
corner; a platform where only sellers reassure each other is a
hall of mirrors. The tell is transferable from any street corner
ever built: do the people passing hold money and a want, or do they
pass to look at other people passing? Traffic is not demand. It
must buy something or make contact that buys something, or else it
is weather.

Notice what this law is not. It does not say chase fashion. It does
not say quit your region because a city wafts better in your
imagination, and it does not recommend the moving that severs
community and calls itself strategy. The test is boring and
checkable: where is the demand I want *already standing*, and am I
actually in its path? A queue proves demand. So do unanswered
quotes that took three weeks to get, and response times, and the
second order that comes without asking. Empty is likewise checkable:
a market where every conversation requires explaining the premise
from zero is empty, whatever the landlord says about footfall.

## Read the Traffic Before You Sign

Given the two sieves so far, here is the working sequence Barnum
sketches, in trade language that translates nicely. You have a
vocation (law 9). For that vocation, list the places demand
concentrates: the physical ones (streets, districts, markets,
factories, hospitals, harbor towns) and the modern echoes of them
(industry groups, marketplaces, directories, the referral chains of
one's actual trade). Then, before committing a lease, a career move,
or a year, go and stand where the demand stands and watch it. Count
inquiries. Ask three incumbents how their orders arrive. Walk the
platform and see who answers, how fast, at what price. Barnum's
generation asked the same thing of a store front that you should ask
of a page: who passes, and do they stop? An evening of this costs
nothing and has saved fortunes.

Two founding questions keep the visit honest, and they are worth
writing on your hand like a shopping list. Who here already pays
for the thing, in money, that I want to be paid for? And: if they
stopped paying, why? The first finds demand, and the second prices
it. Where the answers are clear, you are looking at a real market
with rules you can learn. Where both answers come back cloudy, you
are looking at a fad, a subsidy dressed as a business, or somebody's
tax position, and no amount of moving creates a market where
spending has no motive. Demand that exists for a reason survives;
demand that exists for a season deserves to be visited, not married.

The inventory usually produces a result unlike the applicant's
expectation, and that is the value. Half the time the demand is
nearest where you already are, in the client base you have not
asked for referrals, in the neighboring town your pride considered
beneath the business. The other half of the time it stands one move
away: a better shelf in the same shop, a town two provinces over
where your trade is short-handed, a platform your trade abandoned
half-heartedly years ago. Both results are wins. The silent third
result, staying where nothing walks, is the only loss, and it hides
best behind virtue talk about loyalty and patience.

## Moving Without Shipwreck

If the audit says move, move like a tradesman and not like a
commando. Barnum's own career is a decent map, because he moved
often and lost little in transit. The moves that pay keep the
calling intact (the vocation stays, the location shifts), keep one
income line running through the transition (old clients served
while the new shelf is stocked), and treat move-cost as a real line
item: deposits, weeks of dust, the loss of known suppliers, the
children's schools, all of it priced before the decision, not
discovered after. The moves that ruin are the theatrical ones: burn
nothing until the new spot has produced its first receipts.

Household readers will notice the audit covers them differently,
and that is by design. Location decisions for the employed usually
come down to one question with two honest answers: does my
employer's growth (or my industry's demand) have a future within
commuting distance, and is the answer a fact or a hope? If it is a
fact, the correct move is law 16's systematic growth right where
the family stands: the town pays because the work pays, and the
next decade is stable enough to build on. If it is a hope, the
audit has not failed; it has found your town out early, while a
planned move is still cheap, and the schools, dinners, and
percentages all get to be part of the pricing rather than the
surprise. The family that decides together with the numbers open
has a hard month and a good decade. The family that decides after
the layoff has the reverse, item for item.

Note the one stand-still result worth keeping; nobody is telling
you your tired town is your enemy. If the audit shows your demand
is local and present (because the town is small, but your trade is
one shop for three counties), then the audit just told you to stand
still with confidence and stop apologizing for your address.
