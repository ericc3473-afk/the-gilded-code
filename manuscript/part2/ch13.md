# Use the Best Tools, Including People

Barnum's sixth rule sounds like shop talk and turns out to be a rule
about people wearing the costume of shop talk. You cannot have too
good tools to work with, he says, and of all the tools an enterprise
uses, the ones you should be most particular about are the living
ones. A business runs on hands, and hands are abundantly available;
what is scarce is the hand attached to a head that solves problems
without being walked through them. His age phrased the idea as
hiring; today the same law governs everything you buy to do your
work, every system you install, and every associate, employee,
contractor, or partner you attach to your plans, including,
repeatedly and unavoidably, yourself.

Start with the familiar end, the physical tools, because the
arithmetic there is easy to demonstrate. In every trade there is a
cheap version of the necessary instrument and a good one, and the
cheap version costs more, always, for reasons that only show up in
the ledger after the return window closes. The budget router that
chatters delivers rougher work that sells for less and costs
material. The secondhand van that spends every third Friday at a
garage quietly bills you the purchase price of the good one,
distributed as inconvenience. The free software that drops an
afternoon a month into reformatting and re-exporting is the most
expensive software in the market. Under-buying presents itself as
thrift, and it is actually a loan you take out against your own
margins, at interest collected in lost hours.

## The Thinking Fingers

Barnum passes along an old pulpit illustration of what the best
living tool looks like, and it is too perfect not to keep. An
employer interviews candidates. The first offers a pair of hands,
with one finger that thinks. Good, says the employer, that is
something. The next has two thinking fingers. Better. The third
reports that all his fingers and thumbs think, and the employer
leans in. The last candidate simply says it outright: I have a
brain that thinks; I think all over; I am a thinking as well as a
working man. And that, says the story, is the man you hire. The
point survives every equipment upgrade since: the difference between
labor and the best labor is the amount of the person that comes
along with the body. Any hiring, any subcontracting, any large
purchase of somebody's hours should be priced against that story.
Hands cost the going rate. The brain attached to hands costs more
and earns more, and the gap between the two is the cheapest margin
in most trades.

## Keep the Good Ones

The rule that follows is Barnum's and it is firm: when you get a
good one, keep them. A good person learns every day, and the
learning accrues silently inside your operation; you benefit from
their experience whether you thought to purchase it or not. They
are worth more to the enterprise this year than last year,
automatically, which makes them the last people to part with. There
is a caveat, and Barnum, who employed many hundreds of people over
decades, draws it with a straight edge. If the person begins to
price themselves as invaluable, demanding beyond reason on the
theory that you cannot proceed without them, let them test that
theory somewhere else. Not from spite; from arithmetic and even
kindness, because a person who believes they cannot be spared has
stopped growing, and the enterprise that believes it cannot
proceed without one person has stopped being safe. Keep the best at
reasonable, advancing rates. Do not keep the person who spends
energy proving the place would collapse without them.

The same principle faces inward, which is the uncomfortable
application. You are the leading tool of your own operation, and
the law applies to you first: sharpen the head, keep the habits
good, advance at reasonable rates, and do not let your own sense of
indispensability freeze the machine you run. A reader working alone
should notice that this chapter has now recommended buying better
equipment, sharpening yourself, and surrounding yourself with
people whose fingers think. That is the whole instruction. The
quality of the input instruments and input people sets a ceiling on
the work that no effort penetrates for long.

## Buying Back an Hour

The solo reader eventually meets the law from the other side: not
as somebody's employer, but as the person deciding whether to
become one. A starter question works better than the general
anxiety about hiring, and it is the same test run on your own
hours. For a week, log what your working hours actually go to.
Some hours generate money directly, some maintain the instruments
that generate money, and a third category, which the log will name
for you, neither generates nor maintains anything so much as
absorbs: data re-typed between programs, form letters answered with
form letters, the recurring bookkeeping errand that eats a Tuesday
morning every week. The first hire, in any
trade, is rarely a brilliant one; it is somebody competent at the
absorbing category, on a modest package, bought specifically to
convert those hours into your generating category. Barnum's advice
about hiring good hands applies at any scale, including a
competent person for ten hours a week, because the arithmetic is
the point, not the headcount.

Two rules keep the first buy sound. Hire for the
absorbing category all the way, resisting the temptation to buy
half of somebody brilliant, since fractional brilliant people
produce fractional brains and full invoices. And keep the human
tool afterward by the same standard Barnum set: advance them as
they grow rather than asking them to stay cheap, because an
assistant who has been underpaid into resentment is a system
accident waiting for a quiet Saturday.

## The Upgrade Ledger

Since tools renew constantly and budgets are finite, run the
decision through one ledger rather than in moods. For any tool in
your working life, physical or human or systematic, estimate three
numbers: what the cheap option loses per month (hours, defects,
rework, sanded-off reputation), what the good option costs per
month spread over its life, and who else in your market is
operating with the better instrument. When the first number exceeds
the second, the upgrade is not an aspiration; it is a schedule. When
it does not, buy the cheap one contentedly and spend the difference
elsewhere, because the opposite vice, buying top spec where it does
nothing, is the same disease wearing a nicer coat. Barnum's line is
about adequacy plus ceilings: he tells tradesmen to buy in the
measure of their work, and to raise the measure when the work
rises.

One honest caution closes the chapter, because this rule sits near
where a hundred marketers camp. The best tools doctrine does not
mean the newest tools doctrine. A manual camera, a paid-off saw, a
notebook, a disciplined plain spreadsheet: these have
out-earned shinier rivals in every decade including this one. The
test is never novelty or brand. The test is whether the instrument
raises the ceiling of what you can offer, or merely washes the floor
you already offer. Tools that polish the floor are consumption;
tools that raise the ceiling are capital, and chapter 4's owner
frame already taught you which category compounds.
