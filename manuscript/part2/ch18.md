# Make the Public Look Your Way

Every working person trades with the public, Barnum begins, and he
means the list broadly: lawyers, doctors, shoemakers, artists,
showmen, and college professors all sell something to somebody
outside themselves. From that shared fact he draws the rule almost
nobody needs convincing of and almost everyone under-funds: when
you have an article that genuinely satisfies, let the fact be
known, because however good the article is, if nobody knows of it
it will bring no return. His farm framing makes the economics
plain: sow, then reap. The farmer plants, then attends to other
work, and the harvest comes on schedule. Nobody reaps first and
sows after. Advertising, honestly for a genuine article, is
sowing, and the seed season is always earlier than the hungry
season feels.

He adds a warning against stopping halfway that is funnier than
it is kind. A man had spent his last dollar on drink and asked a
gentleman for ten more, since, he explained, ten cents more of
whiskey would finish the job and thereby save the dollar already
spent. Advertising, he says, works the same and fails the same:
the man who advertises only until the first quiet month and then
stops has lost the whole investment, because the public must know
who and what you are before a single dollar of the sowing pays.
Half-remembered is a category the market does not buy.

He reserved his bluntest line for those who lean on their
quality alone, confident that excellence needs no herald: if a
man has goods for sale and does not advertise them in some way,
the chances are that one day the sheriff will do it for him. The
sentence is a joke with a coroner's report attached. The excellent
unknown does not usually fail with a bang; he fails the way a
shop in the wrong light does, steadily, politely, and season by
season, and the auction that inventories his tools is the first
wide publicity his work ever receives. Whatever else an
advertising budget is, it is cheaper than that announcement, and
it arrives while the goods are still on the shelves.

## The Nine-Dollar-Fifty Hat

His example is the best commercial story of the century, and it
illustrates exactly what attention-unto-reputation looks like.
When the celebrated singer's first American concert was announced,
the first ticket was sold at auction, and a hatter bought it for
two hundred and twenty-five dollars, because he understood what
the purchase would say. Who is the bidder, asked the auctioneer,
and the answer, a hatter, ran through a hall full of strangers.
Nobody present had heard of him by breakfast. By the next
evening the newspapers had carried the item across the country,
and men took off their hats at the news wondering whether they
were wearing one of his. In one town the single owner of a worn
and worthless example showed it in triumph; a neighbor envied
him; someone proposed auctioning it as a keepsake, and it sold
for nine dollars and fifty cents, a better price at that point,
note, than the two cents the hat itself was worth. The consequence for the hatter: ten
thousand extra pairs of his hats per annum for the first six
years, nine-tenths bought from curiosity, and most of those
buyers remaining because, this is the hinge, the hats were good.
The purchase bought attention. The article converted it. Either
half alone breaks.

The companion story is smaller and teaches the same law from the
cheap seats. A tradesman hung a sign over his walk whose message
was, in plain letters: do not read the other side. Of course
everybody read it, and everybody then turned the board over and
read the other side too, which was the point; the man who hung it
made an independence first attracting the public that way, and
then, second clause that completes the law, using his customers
well afterward.

## Politeness Is Capital

Because the second clause is where most advertising quietly dies,
Barnum's rule for the customer counter belongs in the same
chapter. Politeness and civility, he holds, are the best capital
ever invested in business. Large stores, gilt signs, and flaming
advertisements all prove unavailing if the staff behind them
treat patrons abruptly: like begets like, and the more kind and
liberal a merchant is, the more generous the patronage he
receives. The shopman who drives a sharp bargain, acting as
though he never expects to see the customer again, will not be
mistaken; he will never see that customer again, since people
will not pay and be kicked. Underneath it sits his version of the
golden rule for commerce: give the greatest amount of goods of a
corresponding quality for the least sum, still reserving a fair
profit, and customers will do better by you than any extraction
scheme ever did.

The usher story shows the rule running the other way. An attendant
at Barnum's museum, insulted by a visitor, announced his intention
of whipping the man as he left. Barnum's ruling on the spot, in
effect: nothing that visitor said could relieve him of his
ticket, and nothing the usher did in temper could keep the
thousands behind him coming; the public's money carries no duty
to admire you, and your temper spends your own capital. Service
is the theater where reputation, once bought by advertising, is
actually earned or spent.

## Today's Ink

None of the mechanics have moved in a hundred and forty years;
the channels rotated. Where his contemporaries bought print
inches in editions of thousands, you have a page, a listing, a
sign, a neighborhood post, and the inbox of every person your
work has ever satisfied, and most of those are free until you
consider the cost of consistency. The modern split of his
categories runs about like this. The sign and the window became
the page: the named site, the updated listing, the shelf of work
samples a stranger can inspect in ninety seconds. Print became
the inbox and the feed, whose advantage is identical to his
newspaper's: it goes into the family and works while you are
asleep, and whose price is the same as ever, small and constant.
And his curiosity purchase, the two hundred and twenty-five
dollar ticket, survives under many costumes: the local team's
jersey, the fair booth, the sponsorship small enough to be a
joke and large enough to be remembered, the stunt that costs a
day and says the name. Every one of them obeys the same audit as
his: did the outlay put the name in front of people who buy
hats, and did the hats hold them afterward? His exemptions survive as good sense:
a few professions reach their public better by visible works
than by notices, and every one of them still needs to be known
somehow. His severity also survives: advertise spurious goods
and half this chapter turns on you, permanently. But the rule
for the genuine tradesman is untouched by time. Sow before the
harvest. Spend small and steadily rather than large and once.
Make the public look, then give them reason to return without
the sign.
