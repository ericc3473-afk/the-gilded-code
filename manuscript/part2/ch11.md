# Debt Bites Slow and Holds Hard

Barnum's third law is the first one written in the voice of a man
pointing at his own scars. He tells it as a young man's story of a
new suit: someone barely out his teens walks into a shop and gets
"trusted" for a suit of clothes. He struts out. He even invites his
friend to admire the credit, chalking it up to victory: look what my
smile can do. And here is Barnum's dry annotation: the young man got
clothes for his smile, and spent the following year worrying about
how the tailor's book would read. From then on the suit decides
several of his decisions. When the tailor passes by, or the ledger
is mentioned, the young fellow's posture slides, an old plain-suit
walk returns, and the smile turns selective.

It is a small story deliberately, because debt never begins with a
tornado. It begins with something the size of a suit, approved in
ninety seconds, and it behaves like the process of growing a vine on
your porch. The vine advertises itself as decoration. Within two
years the servants' entrance of your decisions has a new landlord:
the car choice, the job-change courage, the speaking-up at council,
even the walk past the shop window, all quietly taking their
instructions from a monthly note. Barnum's word for the final state
is slavery, family slavery, and he is not being florid. His
practical case is the indebted father of a household who once
provided on his own terms and now provides under signatures, while
the losing choices pile up where the household cannot see them.

## How Debt Buys Its Power

Understanding the mechanism makes the case better than slogans. A
lifestyle debt, credit on things you'd buy anyway because the
calendar says you should have them now, subtracts in four ways at
once. It charges interest, which everyone counts. It charges
flexibility, which everyone undercounts: a debtor cannot quit, move,
wait out a bad season, or take the honest low offer, because the
monthly note is uninterviewable. It charges dignity, which is
visible everywhere in the accounts: where the debtor smiles, what
calls he answers, which rooms he skips. And it charges the future's
decisions, which is the cruelest: poverty of imagination is rarely
chosen; it is often rented, by the person whose every free thought
has been spent on the arithmetic of the note.

There's one more purchase that the sting hides best, and it is the
family one. Debts in a household arrive with an etiquette: nobody
mentions them, the children learn not to ask, and the skills nobody
uses get lost as skills. The children of debtor households often
grow up fluent in two dialects: "we're fine" and "we don't discuss
that," and both dialects read as virtue right up until the day
they're needed as arithmetic. Households that keep their accounts
conversational raise adults better at money. Families that keep the
subject behind the sugar bowl raise adults who inherit the silence
first and the numbers second.

## The Modern Serpents

The suits of 1880 now arrive wearing better packaging, so name the
current disguises. The "affordable monthly" is the old chalk ledger
with a nicer clerk: the denominator hides the price and calls the
fraction freedom. Buy-now-pay-later is the same bar with a shorter
chain, and its true function is to let the household spend a
paycheck before it has been received, times four. The trade-up car
payment, renewed every third year out of habit, is a subscription to
somebody else's interest income. The card balance borrowed at
Friday's rate to maintain a lifestyle financed as if it were
business equipment is the most common single case, because
businesses buy tools that produce; a wage-earner's lunch calendar
has never produced anything.

One more serpent worth naming because it advertises itself as the
cure for the others: the consolidation loan. Sometimes it is real
medicine, one lower rate replacing four higher ones, fees counted
and cards actually closed. More often it is the vine repotted. The
balances roll into one comfortable note, the cleared cards are
immediately reused because the lifestyles that created them were
never addressed, and within two years the household services both
the loan and the new balances, which is the original debt wearing a
smaller costume and twice the company. If the consolidation is run,
run it with the cards closed and the sixty-day ritual already on
the calendar, or it is not a cure; it is a transfer.

None of this means a loan is villainous as an object. Barnum's
countrymen financed shops and ships with loans willingly, and so
should you when the arithmetic holds. A debt can be tolerated under
one honest test: does it buy something that compounds without your
daily labor (the van that carries the jobs, the workshop building,
the training that changes what you can charge), and does the
generator it buys produce enough to service the note with a margin
left over, in a bad season, not just a good one? Then the debt is
equipment, like the saw. Lifestyle credit fails this test on sight:
it buys consumption and charges you premium rent for the privilege
of having it three months early. A wage paid to the time-value of a
sofa is a strange purchase, and it is being bought thousands of
times a day.

## The Standing Rules, and the Ladder for Those Already In

Rules for avoiding the bite, stated once and briefly. Pay as you go:
if the thing cannot be bought this month, it obtains this month's
size, not next year's. Finance only the compounding, and only with
margin. Keep the household's accounts conversational, the numbers on
the table, the subjects above the sugar bowl. And treat every
installment offer that arrives as a price to be computed: what
total? For how many years? At what rate? Barnum's advice has no
year on it because the arithmetic hasn't changed.

Design one ritual so the rules run themselves, and the useful length
is sixty days. Twice a household quarter, sit with every obligation
on one page: balances, rates, minimums, and what each debt bought.
The session costs twenty minutes and does three jobs. It catches new
borrowing while it is still small enough to reverse without drama.
It hands the household one shared page to talk over, which is the
conversation most families are missing. And it turns the abstract
idea "we should avoid debt" into a habit with a date on the
calendar, which is the only form in which any advice in this book
survives contact with a busy year. Sixty-day pages, kept honestly,
watch your credit file better than any subscription service, and
they never sell you anything.

For readers already carrying lifestyle debt, diagnosis first, then
the ladder. List every note: amount, rate, what it bought. Mark
each lifestyle or asset. The asset ones stand. The lifestyle ones
are the patient. Then run the standard two-step ladder in the open,
visible to the household: smallest balance first, or highest rate
first, whichever keeps you crossing days off (both are respectable;
the disputed part is psychology, and the psychology that pays is
the one you finish). Adjust the cards. One written note per lender;
a quiet renegotiation is a legitimate act of commerce, not begging.
And stop taking on new ones during the climb, since adding weights
to a lifter is a strange fitness program. The shame can be skipped
entirely: it pays no interest and changes no balances.
