# The Whole Code in One Hand

Wattles ends his book by refusing to add anything, which is the
most honest thing an ending can do, and this chapter follows his
example. What follows is the whole of his summary, translated
into this book's own vocabulary without a single new idea, then
the whole code as it now stands, and the thirty days that begin
tomorrow.

Translated, his summary runs as follows. There is a way of
holding your attention that reliably produces wealth, because a
person who pictures a definite richer life, with faith and
thankfulness renewed daily, genuinely recalibrates what they
notice, ask, attempt, and choose; nobody can run a wealth plan
from the competitive mind, which sees every good as another's
lost share, but only from the creating mind, which produces
value that did not exist before. What is pictured arrives, when
it arrives, through the ordinary channels of trade and
commerce, which is his most under-quoted sentence and the one
that keeps the whole system sane: nothing supernatural, no
windfall, the goods arrive by the same mail as everyone
else's. To receive his own when it comes, the person must be
active; and the activity consists in doing more than merely
filling the present place: keeping the purpose in mind, doing
every day all that can be done that day, each act done in a
successful manner, and leaving every counterpart a use value in
excess of the cash taken. And the riches received will be in
exact proportion to the definiteness of the vision, the fixity
of purpose, the steadiness of faith, and the depth of
gratitude. Four dials, and the science, whole, at one reading.

Wattles was sure enough of the machine to make a prediction at
the exact spot where most authors hedge, and it deserves to be
quoted once for the reader who wants to know what will happen
if this is run without shortcuts: those who practice these
instructions, he says, will certainly get rich, and the size of
the riches will be in exact proportion to the definiteness of
the vision, the fixity of purpose, the steadiness of faith, and
the depth of gratitude. Not approximately, not aspirationally,
in exact proportion. He was right about the kind of claim to be
wary of, too, and it is important to read the sentence with the
same care your own checkbook gets. The proportionality is a
claim about attention held daily and acts done thoroughly,
which is to say that the dials measure the practice as well as
reward it. A reader who runs the system at a quarter of its
daily dose gets, on his own terms, the quarter's worth, and
blames science for the arithmetic. The proportionality of law 2
was what his chapter on exactness promised, and this is the
closing restatement: the machine is only as strong as how
often you sit down and switch it on.

## Why the Code Has Hinges

A reader arriving here without the middle of the book will read
the twenty-two lines as good advice and feel nothing, which is
correct, and it is worth explaining why the code is shaped as a
structure and not as a list. The laws divide into three
load-bearing assemblies. The thinking assembly, laws one
through eight, is the engine: without a permitted want, a held
picture, and a fenced mind, the doing chapters feel like chores
and quietly get rescheduled forever by an ambivalent
administration. The doing assembly, laws nine through
eighteen, is the drive train: invoices, hours, tools, and
customers, where money is actually housed, and where no amount
of good thinking substitutes for the paying of attention on
Tuesday. The conduct assembly, nineteen through twenty-one, is
the frame: reputation, fit, and integrity, which keep the
machine roadworthy past the first bad season. And this final
law is the driver's bench, where the assemblies meet a
schedule. Remove any one assembly and the car still looks
exactly like a car, which is why so many money books look
exactly like this one; the difference is not the parts. It is
whether the parts are all installed.

## The Code, in Twenty-Two Lines

For the log's front page, one line per law, the shortest true
form of each:

1. You are allowed to want money; it is the wish for more life.
2. Wealth runs on law and learnable conduct, not luck.
3. Opportunity is not monopolized; somewhere a door is open.
4. Attend like an owner: build what survives the workweek.
5. See the exact life you intend, with dates, before it exists.
6. Run the gratitude switch: name what works, daily.
7. Mind your own mind; the will guards your attention, not theirs.
8. Do all that can be done today, in whole, efficient acts.
9. Choose work your nature can stand; give taste a trial.
10. Put yourself where the demand already stands.
11. Refuse lifestyle debt; pay as you go.
12. Whatever is worth doing is worth your whole self, now.
13. Buy the best tools, including people, and keep the good ones.
14. Serve your trade, learn something useful; no royal road.
15. Hope much, bet little; hammer one nail per season.
16. Run a time and place for everything; read your trade's news.
17. No side bets beyond what you can spare; never sign without security.
18. Make the public look your way for something genuine.
19. Leave everyone a little better; advance quietly.
20. Get into the right business, by growth and by fit.
21. Keep your integrity; it is the fortune that cannot be lost.
22. Carry the whole code daily: the thought, the act, the honesty.

Twenty-two checks on a page. Nothing on it requires a library,
a license, or a loan, and each line has, somewhere behind it in
this book, a chapter that showed the mechanism and a log line
that proves whether you ran it.

## The Next Thirty Days

The first lap is the only one that changes anything, because
habits are not installed by conclusions. Machine it this way,
exactly as the front of the book promised. Each day: read the
day's law and its opener line from the page in the back matter;
perform its do-now in whatever version you are running; write
the one-track line; place tomorrow's law in your path. Thirty
days, twenty-two laws, with the surplus days spent finishing
what the heavier laws opened. At the lap's end, run the audit
page in the back matter: rate each law for how it ran, choose
the two you will build the next lap around, and start again.
Four laps is the year's work; the fourth is when people with
the eight-month habit start calling it luck.

The last thing this book asks of you is the thing the first
chapter asked. Permission is not a mood but a practice, and
you now hold every practice the two books contain. The showman
escapes the poorhouse by observation; the quiet Midwesterner by
holding a picture through a rainy season; you by the same
route: the definite picture, the fenced morning, the paid-down
ledger, the tools kept sharp, the public told, the deals clean,
the name good for credit, and Tuesday's small act done whole.
Get tomorrow's law read tonight, and the money will meet you
in the doing.
