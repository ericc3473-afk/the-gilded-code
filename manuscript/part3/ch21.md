# Integrity Is the Fortune That Cannot Be Lost

The last of Barnum's rules is also the shortest sentence of his
creed: it is more precious than diamonds or rubies. He is talking
about integrity, and he argues for it the way he argues about
everything, from the showman's ledger outward, beginning with the
stupidest advice ever given. The old miser told his sons, get
money; get it honestly if you can, but get money. Barnum calls
that advice atrociously wicked, and then, the sharper cut, the
very essence of stupidity, and he is right in an arithmetic sense
the moralists often miss. The saying quietly assumes that honest
money is a constraint and dishonest money a shortcut. The truth
runs the other way. Making money dishonestly is the most
difficult thing in life, because the whole arrangement
administers leaks in every direction: the deed must be hidden
from everyone whose cooperation the scheme needs, the record
must be maintained against people who do not need the money and
cannot be trusted to need silence, and the discovery, he says
flatly, is near-universal sooner or later. Prisons are full of
followers of the miser's advice, and when the want of principle
is found out, nearly every avenue to success closes at once,
permanently, across trades.

## False Weights and Measures

The reason closes every door at once rather than a few, and the
mechanism is suspicion, not morality. The public, Barnum says,
very properly shuns all whose integrity is doubted; no matter how
polite and pleasant and accommodating a person may be, none of us
dares deal with the one suspected of false weights and measures.
The honest trade, once doubted, is asymmetric in exactly the way
that punishes forever, because doubt does not usually announce
itself: customers doubt quietly, tell nobody, and simply stop
coming, and the merchant whose scale reads two percent light
never receives the one report that would have let him fix it. By
contrast, the person known to be strictly honest, however poor
today, has the purses of the whole community available to him,
since everyone knows that a promise to repay from that person is
the same as repayment. Integrity, on the ledger, is the credit
line nobody can invoice you for and nobody can cap.

The other half of the case Barnum draws from inside the lived
experience, which no other pitch can: it secures its
possessor a peace that cannot be purchased; no amount of money,
houses, and lands buy back the squared account. Readers do not
need assurances on this. Every person who has carried a
concealment for a season already knows the interest rate, and
nobody has ever collected a windfall from it.

## What Cannot Be Stolen

There is one more asymmetry, and it completes the warning into a
comfort. A fortune that was acquired honestly, even after the
hardest of luck, retains its own tools: the skill that made it,
the name that cooperated with the market, the account books
nobody has to audit twice. Such a fortune can be lost and
rebuilt, which is precisely what happened in the era of the two
books we love: a famous bankrupt showman spent a half-decade
paying every creditor in full and then assembled a larger
fortune than the first one, with the same name, in public, and
the secret that made it possible is exactly this chapter. People
he had dealt with straight for thirty years knew the robbery had
come from the market and not from him, and backed his name
accordingly. Now invert it, since some reader will want to: a
fortune that was gotten crookedly cannot be defended, because a
fortune is not cash but a probability field of future
cooperation, and the discovery of the false weights deletes the
key assumption in every future transaction at once. The
dishonest wealth is owned till the first audit; the honest
reputation is owned in exactly the way your next race is
possible at all. That is what Barnum meant by calling the
honest-getting harder: it is the only getting that compounds,
and the other kind does not even keep.

## The Customer Who Can All See

One modern note presses the whole law tighter than Barnum could,
and it runs in your favor. His scale problem, the suspicion that
never announces itself and quietly empties the shop, is mostly
solved in your favor now, since a bad-faith record of the small
kind, the buried fee, the padded invoice, the quietly worsened
second mile, is discoverable by the next twenty customers at the
touch of a page, and its spread rate outruns any advertising
budget aimed at repairing it. This is terrifying for the
corner-cutter and, for someone running clean, it is the best
news in the market: the honest now have instruments their
predecessors lacked, reviews, retained quotes, dated
photographs, published prices, that make false weights
expensive to hide in a way no earlier century could offer. The
law's instruction is unchanged. Its enforcement schedule simply
accelerated, which is a discount to the honest and a terminus to
everyone else.

## The Competitive Plane

Wattles finishes his book with the ideas that protect the whole
science from its misuses, and the first caution is aimed straight
at the temptation this chapter exists for. When a person's
supplies of things seem limited, and every good deal looks like
something taken from somebody else, the mind has slid onto what
he calls the competitive plane, and there a person is fighting
for shares while other people fight the same, which is where
false weights, quiet shortcuts, and every grift in the book live. On the
plane of creation, the one this book has been teaching from part
one, wealth is produced by making better things for people who
want them, and its supply is not of a fixed stock to loot. His
assessment is blunt enough to repeat: the more people who get
rich on the competitive plane, the worse for everybody, and the
more who get rich creating, the better for everybody. So the
integrity instruction is not a garnish on the plan. It is a
geography lesson: dishonesty and corner-cutting are not sins
adding risk to the way, they are the address of the old, crowded,
losing plane, and the whole structure of this code stands on the
other side of the street.

His companion cautions serve the same construction, and they
belong in your log with the rest. Guard your speech: do not speak
of yourself, your affairs, the world, or the times in the
discouraging voice, because speech in that voice is the fastest
round-trip a doubt can take back to your own intentions. Expect
what can actually arrive by the actual date, and when a deal
overrules, refuse disappointment as data only: the record of his
case study is precise, a wanted combination fails at the
critical hour, the reader goes on grateful, and weeks later a
better arrangement arrives that the failed one would have
entangled away. None of this is a promise that every closed door
is secretly a blessing. It is instruction about the posture at
the door: the person who goes on in full faith, purpose, and
gratitude, doing the day's work perfectly and without haste,
cannot be defeated by combinations of circumstances in general,
only delayed within them.
