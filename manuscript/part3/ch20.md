# Get Into the Right Business

Wattles' chapter on choosing your business starts where a
reader of Part I can guess: with a distinction. Success in any
particular work depends on possessing, in a decently developed
state, the faculties that work requires, and possessing them does
not guarantee wealth. There are musicians of remarkable talent
who remain poor; blacksmiths and carpenters with excellent
mechanical ability who never get free of the wage; merchants with
a real gift for dealing with men who fail anyway. The faculties
are tools. It is essential to have good tools, and it is equally
essential to use them in the right way: one person takes a sharp
saw, a square, and a good plane, and builds a handsome piece of
furniture, and a second takes the identical tools and produces a
botch. So the search for the right business is a search for the
place where your particular set of tools, held right way up, does
its work at its actual advantage.

## Fit, Want, and the Proof of Desire

Two selection rules divide the question neatly. Generally,
he says, you will do best in the business that uses your
strongest faculties, the one you are naturally fitted for; but
you will get rich most easily in point of effort that way, and
most satisfactorily by doing what you want to do. Doing what you
want to do is life, and there is no real satisfaction in being
forever compelled at a thing you do not like. Then the argument
makes its most quotable move, and it is worth keeping exactly:
desire is power announcing itself. Where there is no power at
all, developed or undeveloped, there is never any desire to do a
thing; the wish to play music is the music in you seeking
expression, and the wish to build is the building in you looking
for a site. So a strong, non-fading desire for a line of work is
admissible evidence that the line is available to you. All else
equal, take the well-fitted trade; but if a particular work keeps
calling across years, treat the call as data about capacity, and
aim the whole plan at it.

The pairing resolves the fight this book refuses to host. Law 9,
Barnum's vocation rule, said choose work your nature can stand;
this chapter answers the reader who heard that and despaired of
their one passion, by pointing out that fit is developable. There
is no talent, Wattles says flatly, of which you do not have at
least the rudiment; lacking a developed faculty for the wanted
work means making your tools as you go instead of using only the
ones you were born with, which is slower and not forbidden.

## Making the Interim Desk Livable

For the reader stuck at a desk that is merely the funding stage
for a better one, Wattles supplies a piece of emotional
engineering that outlasts inspiration. Past mistakes may
currently confine you to work you do not like; you may be
obliged, for some time, to keep doing it; and the trick that
makes the interim whole is carried entirely by what you know the
doing is for. The unloved desk that funds the training, connects
to the future trade's first clients, and holds the household
steady while the rudimentary faculty grows is a different object
from the unloved desk that leads nowhere, though the pay stubs
are identical. One is a season on the road. The other is a
sentence in a cell. The difference is entirely in the plan it
connects to, which returns every reader to law 5: without a held
picture, drudgery is just wall; with one, the same hours pay as
road. Nobody should pretend the liking arrives from attitude
alone. What arrives is patience, which is wanting-with-a-date,
and patience under those conditions is the trait that separates
the career built in two moves from the two lives spent on one.

## Change by Growth, Not by Leap

The escape procedure for the wrong business is where most
earnest people preventable-ruin themselves, and Wattles
writes it as a sequence. If you are not in the right vocation,
do not act too hastily in trying to get into another. The best
way to change business or environment, generally, is by
growth. Do not be afraid of a sudden radical change if the
right opportunity presents itself and careful consideration
confirms it; but never make sudden, radical action when in
doubt, because mistakes come from acting hastily, or in fear
or doubt, or in forgetfulness of the motive that has carried
this whole book: more life to all, and less to none. A day or
two of steadied vision and honest thanksgiving, in his system,
makes for better decisions than any amount of contracted
staring at a deadline that fear built.

There is a seasonal modesty in the rule that deserves
translation, because it answers every midnight impulse to burn
the shop down. When you get out of the competitive frame, you
never need to act hastily; no one is going to beat you to the
thing you want, there is room for more of it than one person
will occupy, and if one place is taken, another opens a little
farther on. Plenty of time is the quiet assumption underneath
every good career decision, and hurry, in the language of this
book's first part, is a leak, not an engine: the moment a
decision is rushed by fear, its average drops below the same
decision taken ninety days later by the same person with a
better calendar. The reader escaping a job not yet outgrown,
holds law 9's instruction with chapter 14's: keep the paying
desk, learn something useful toward the wanted desk, and let
the change grow rather than tear.

## The Coupled Test

Since Part III exists to weld the halves, state the chapter as
the whole test a candidate business must pass. One, fit: the
strongest available faculties, or a stated plan for developing
the missing one, priced in real practice hours. Two, want: a
desire that has survived a year of contact with the work's
unglamorous middle, since desire that only exists at the start
of things is novelty renting the word. Three, capacity for
increase: a way the work leaves people better, per chapter
19, and a buyer somewhere willing and able to pay for it, per
chapter 10. Four, tools: the instruments, from chapter 13, that
the better version of the work assumes, budgeted and scheduled.
A wish that has passed all four is no longer a wish. It is a
business in pre-inventory, and the next thirty days are about
opening the books.

Two closing cautions round the law, one for each direction the
reader may be leaning. First, for the restless: the coupled test
is slow on purpose, and a candidate who feels rushed has usually
confused the fear of missing out with a desire, which is why the
test requires a year of contact before the want's vote counts.
Second, for the stuck: the test is not a leash, and law 9's
misplaced-hotel-man lives in every decade. A business that
repeatedly fails the test with no plausible growth plan toward
passing it is not an interim; it is the thing itself, and the
honest response is the planned move, funded by the current desk
and timed by the calendar rather than by fear. Deciding which
kind of stuck you are, interim or permanent, is this chapter's
real do-now, and the log will tell you the truth if the log is
honest.
