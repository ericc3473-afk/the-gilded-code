# Leave Everyone a Little Better

Part III begins where the two books finally lean together. Wattles,
who has spent his whole argument on the inside of your head, turns
here to the only other person in the transaction: everyone you deal
with. His instruction is a single compound phrase worth adopting as
a career title: convey the impression of increase. People are all
seeking more life, he says, more skill, more comfort, more beauty,
more room, whatever more means in their season, and the person who
deals with them in a way that visibly feeds that seeking will be
sought out from anywhere. The desire for increase is the standing
weather of every customer you will ever have. You are not selling
mostly a thing; you are selling a direction, and the direction is
up.

The content of the impression matters more than the showing of it.
Wattles is exact here: the advancing woman actually advances.
Everything she does is done with the purpose of adding to the sum
of life, over and above whatever she receives for the adding. The
carpenter builds a better door than was asked for and does not bill
the betterment as a surprise; the physician sees each patient a
degree more restored, and the news of that restoration is carried
by patients, not by the physician; the clerk who leaves every desk
she touches in improved order is on the shortest list in any office
for the bigger desk. Notice that this is chapter 14's law on
quality plus a social half strangers can actually feel. Doing well
is the first stage. Being sensible of it is the second, and the
second is the one people forget, since nobody hires what they
cannot perceive.

## The Advancement Audit

The working version of this law is an audit of your last three
transactions, and it is uncomfortable in exactly the way audits
that work always are. For each person you recently did business
with, write down what they gained besides the thing they paid for:
faster service than promised, a referral they did not expect, the
cheaper option honestly recommended, the lesson embedded in the
delivery, the phone answered at the third attempt and afterward.
Where the column comes up empty, with money simply flowing one way
against goods flowing the other, you have found the reason that
referrals are scarce. Nothing here requires unprofitable conduct,
and Wattles' phrase for the arithmetic, more life to all and less
to none, is a price-setting rule rather than a martyr's vow: the
gain you leave must be real, and the price you keep must be fair,
and the two are the whole instruction.

Run the audit inside the household too, because the law has no
office hours. The employer at home is also a buyer and a seller:
the spouse's day gains or loses by whatever impression your
evening conveys, the children run their own looking-for-more on
whatever model of increase the kitchen shows them, and the
friendship with the sibling who called twice this month has
clearly been financed at the family rate of one-sided attention.
There is no money in the column and no invoice either, which is
why the audit is worth running there first: household increase,
conveyed or withheld, is the first reputation anybody ever builds,
and the one every later reputation grows on.

The advancing frame also refreshes how you treat strangers, since
the impression is conveyed outside invoices too. Wattles gives the
case of the wage-earner and professional directly, and it cuts
both ways of the status ladder: everybody you meet is engaged in
the same looking-for-more, so the freely given praise of a
colleague's progress, the message that tells a supplier they
undercharged, the five minutes of genuine attention that leaves
the teller or apprentice moving more hopefully toward their own
more, all of these are the same conveyance in its cheapest and
most repeatable form. People can be given their sense of increase
and then, again in his phrasing, they will be drawn to you.

One caution guards the whole law from its imitators, because the
impression of increase has been confusion-sold as constant
flattery. Conveying increase is not telling people they are
wonderful; it is leaving evidence they can examine whether or not
you are present. Flattery costs a sentence and is priced at
detection within the week, and the flatterer conveys, for free,
the exact opposite impression: that they see a person to be
handled in place of the person who is there. Barnum's showmanship
is the honest extreme of the same distinction. His exhibits were
often absurd, but the transaction was never artificially priced,
his name was genuinely attached to what he sold, and the
thousands of visitors who came for the elephant left with the
fair minute of wonder they had paid for and did not resent
paying. That is why his audiences returned. The seller's whole
position in this law is simpler than technique: be somebody whose
leavings improve, and then make no effort to hide it.

## Give Quietly, Except to the Seller

Two of Barnum's rules complete the picture, because their subject
is the noise around generosity. The first says be charitable, and
he supports it with every incentive available to a showman,
including the self-interested one: the liberal person commands
patronage, says so, while the sordid one is avoided. His practical
filter survives whole. The best charity helps those struggling to
help themselves; indiscriminate alms without inquiry are bad in
every sense; and giving a benediction instead of bread to the
hungry is the century's most memorable violation. The quiet assist
to the neighbor who is clearly doing the work is charity that
increases even as it scatters.

The second rule is the one that costs nothing and is obeyed by
almost nobody: do not blab. Say nothing about your profits, your
hopes, your expectations, your intentions, and hold the same
discipline in your letters and messages. Nothing is gained by
telling how it was done, and often much is lost, and the
discipline's stranger half is a rule about losing: if you are
losing money, be specially cautious and do not tell of it, or you
will lose your reputation right behind the money.

## What This Buys Across a Decade

It is worth pricing the compound before the chapter closes,
because this law pays in the slow currencies whose receipts are
easy to miss. The reputation for leaving increase returns as
access: people take your calls before your competitors', tell you
the problems before the problems are tendered widely, and forgive
the single bad month that ends every permanent account. It
returns as information, since the tradesman everybody describes
as generous hears the market's movements first, from people who
want the next good thing to reach them equally early. And it
returns at the closing of every bigger future version of your
work, where the references, the farthest networks, and the buyer
with no other candidate are all decided by a decade of small
leavings that no ledger ever printed. Increase conveys; the
account stays open; and nothing in this book works harder while
advertising itself less.

The advancing person and the blabber cannot coexist, because the
blab leaks both directions, crowing the wins until the wins'
audience feels used, and narrating the losses until the losses
become the name itself. Increase communicates; it does not
narrate itself. Let the carpenter's doors, the cured patients,
and the unvarnished quietness of your own ledger do the
accountancy.
