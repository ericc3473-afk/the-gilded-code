# Introduction: Two Showmen, One Pattern

In 1880, P. T. Barnum sat down to explain how he did money. Not the
circus part; the part nobody photographs. He was sixty-nine years old,
a decade past the bankruptcy that had cost him everything, and still
the most famous showman alive. The book he wrote, *The Art of Money
Getting*, contains no compound-interest charts and no pep talks. It
contains rules like avoid debt, read the newspapers, and don't indorse
without security, delivered with the dry patience of a man who had lost
money enough ways to know where the holes are.

Thirty years later, Wallace Wattles published *The Science of Getting
Rich* and then, more or less, disappeared. We know almost nothing about
him: a Midwestern life, ordinary means, a handful of books, an early
death in 1911. His one immortal idea can be said in a line: getting
rich is not a talent, a temperament, or an accident. It works the same
way every time, for anyone who thinks and acts in a certain way, and
you can learn that way. A century later his little book inspired a film
and a shelf of imitators. Most of the imitators kept the promises and
dropped the machinery. Wattles kept the machinery.

One of these two wrote about the mind. One wrote about Tuesday. That is
why they belong together, and why this book exists. Barnum tells you
exactly what to do about your habits and your ledger, and he is almost
silent about what you believe. Wattles rebuilds your thinking from the
studs out, and he is nearly useless about what to do at 9 a.m. on an
ordinary weekday. One of them writes the inner manual, the other the
outer one.

They even share a résumé of failure, which you should know before you
start. In 1856, Barnum lost every dollar he had because he kept signing
his name under other men's loans, friendly paper for a friend's clock
company that turned out to be rotten. Retiring the debt took him years
of lecturing and exhibit-hustling, and he eventually bought his beloved
museum back. He wrote *The Art of Money Getting* long after that
humbling, and its bitterest laws, about debt, about security, about
outside deals, are written by a man describing exactly how he ruined
himself. Wattles, for his part, knew want from the inside; his
biographers find him running short most of his adult life, and his
book reads as a letter written to a version of himself that kept
settling for less. Neither author is selling detachment from the
problem. Both are telling you how they finally solved it.

Here is the pattern both men point at, which this book turns into
twenty-two laws:

> **Money follows a person who wants more of life, sees the bigger life
> clearly before it exists, acts on it every ordinary day, and handles
> every person and every dollar along the way honestly.**

The pattern carries the three parts ahead: the wanting and the seeing
(Part I), the doing (Part II), and the honesty that keeps the whole
thing standing (Part III).

**A map, briefly.** Part I consists of eight laws about the inside of
your head. You will give yourself permission to want (law 1), accept
that wealth runs on law rather than luck (law 2), drop the story that
all the good opportunities are gone (law 3), and learn to hold a clear
picture of the life you intend (laws 4 to 7). It closes with the law
that keeps the other seven honest: all of it must leak into action, not
into daydreaming (law 8). Part II is Barnum's ground, ten laws about
hours, invoices, and people: choosing work that fits your nature,
staying out of debt's slow poison, finishing everything with your whole
self, buying the best tools and keeping the best people, staying
systematic, refusing side bets, and making your name easy to find.
Part III takes the next four hardest: the laws you run on people
(leaving them better off and keeping your profits quiet), the law that
sends you into the right business rather than the convenient one, the
law of integrity that both authors place last, and a closing page that
puts the whole code in one hand for the next thirty days.

No law requires money to start, which is worth saying plainly on the
first page of a book about getting rich. Several of them, run in
reverse for a week, will show you exactly where your leaks are; the
rest build steadily on that. If you ever want to preview the shape of
the machine, the back matter prints all twenty-two laws on one page.

**What this book does with your two ancestors.** Everything here is
rewritten in today's voice, because neither man can speak to you in
his original costume. Barnum's Kings and Emporiums date him, and half
his rules assume a shop and a counter. His shop is now your laptop; his
counter is your calendar. His stories about hotel-keepers and idle
apprentices keep their point. Wattles wrote in a mystical idiom and gave his
reasoning a religious-sounding vocabulary, which is the one thing his
imitators copied most faithfully. I have kept his machinery, his
claims, and his argument, and retuned the words into what they were
actually doing: describing a disciplined way of holding attention that
changes what you notice, what you attempt, and how you treat people.

**A word about age.** You could object that two books from 1880 and
1910 are old news. Consider what has changed since in both fields. The
typography is better, the interest rates are lower, and the machines
are faster, but the shape of a person going from broke to solid has not
changed: notice what pays, want it with your whole attention before it
exists, work on schedule and completely, keep your hands clean and
your mouth shut. Barnum's habits read old because ours wear new shoes.
The one field that needed real translation is his advertising chapter,
where the newspapers he loved are now feeds and inboxes; the catch-all
rule underneath it, put your name where the public can find it, has
never been truer than in an economy where everyone is one click from
being found.

**What this book will honestly do.** No book can make you rich, and
neither of your authors believed in the exception, however loudly his
imitators have promised it since. What a book can do is install a
pattern where one was missing, and these twenty-two laws are the most
durable pattern on the subject I have found anywhere. The work, the
tracking, and the discomfort when a law lands on your worst habit are
still yours. But you are holding the two books that survived a century
of replacements. I have condensed them to about a third of their
combined length and cut everything that had stopped paying rent. What
is left is the code.

**A word on the stories.** A handful of Barnum's anecdotes survived the
cutting, because they still prove what they proved in 1880: the young
man proudly "trusted" for a new suit of clothes, the hatter who spent
$225 on the first Jenny Lind ticket for the advertising, the miser who
advised his sons to get money by any means. Barnum tells several of
them on himself, which is a good part of why the telling works.

**Your first three questions.** Before Part I, write answers to these
in a journal or notes app. Not because anyone will check. Because once
you have answered, the twenty-two laws stop being advice and start
being a report on your own patterns:

1. Where does your money actually leak? Name the three holes. You
   almost know them already.
2. If money were no object, what would you do *first*? Say it, and also
   say exactly which bill or fear stands in front of it.
3. When did you last get something you *asked* for? Write the date.
   One part of this book will ask you for it again.

> **Do this now:** Answer the three questions. A paragraph each is
> plenty.
>
> **Track it:** Put a recurring reminder on your calendar to spend ten
> minutes a day with this book during your first read. The back matter
> explains where the minutes go after that.
