# Think Like an Owner

Two people clock in at the same shop every morning for years. At the
end of the decade one has a margin, a house note falling, and a
sideline that pays for itself; the other has exactly the same history
and a smaller flat. Neither inherited anything; the difference was
never the wage. One of them treated every hour as a piece of capital
granted to an owner. The other treated every hour as a stub to redeem.
At the end of a shift the stub gets cashed and is gone. The capital
one gets deployed and keeps compounding at odd rates, in skills, in
reputation, in equipment, in accounts.

Wallace Wattles builds his whole science on a claim here that,
translated honestly, is the owner's frame. His wording is 1910
metaphysics: everything in the world is made of one underlying stuff,
and that stuff is responsive to thought, so a person who holds a
definite formed picture and acts may bring corresponding things into
being. Dressed for today, without the metaphysics: the world answers
people whose attention is ordered. Attention decides what you notice,
what you attempt, what you buy, what you tolerate, whom you approach,
and what gets built after a month of Tuesdays. Nothing arrives because
a man stared. But staring at one object, in the way this chapter
means, is the front end of arriving: it sorts the mail you open, the
questions you ask, and the room you walk into next.

## Life Presses to Increase

The second half of the principle is simpler and stranger in its
pleasantness: everything alive presses toward more life. The blade of
grass sends a leaf through the sidewalk seam because it can do no
other thing. Your body repairs, your mind connects, your days spend
themselves building something, whether you chose it or not. What Wattles
lights up is the continuity between that pressure and your own wanting.
If the appetite for increase runs through all living things, then the
young man or woman who wants a bigger life is not in moral failure; he
is aligned with the direction of everything that grows. And the
opposite is also true: a person who has quietly capitulated to
smallness is rowing against his own design, which is why the settling
feels so much like sickness. You know the feeling; you have felt it on
long dark afternoons in the wrong job.

Consequently the wants you carried out of the *Right to Be Rich*
chapter are not personal failings to be managed. They are material.
Life presses to increase through you, specifically through what you
choose to grow this season, and your only real freedom of will lies in
which direction you point a pressure that is not optional in the first
place.

## Renting Your Attention or Owning It

Money itself has a renter version and an owner version, and this is
where the frame becomes practical instead of philosophical. The renter
sells attention by the hour: whoever pays gets it, the invoice says
what it was, and the following morning starts from zero. The owner
puts attention into things that survive outside any one workday:
skills that compound, relationships that refer, equipment that earns
while you sleep, accounts that grow on schedule, work samples that
generate requests for years. Both forms are legitimate; the owner
frame says one thing only, which is to place, deliberately and on
purpose, part of every week's attention into the second category.

Test the split against your last ordinary week. Count the hours that
survived you: work samples, notes a future client could commission
from, one instrument learned one degree better, one relationship
warmed rather than merely maintained. Most honest people count
between nothing and two hours out of the hundred they spent awake
outside work, and the rest of those hundred paid rent with no
forward address left behind. There is no guilt in that number; it is
merely the current draft, and drafts get revised.

Notice what is special about this rule: it works at every income.
The employee can own an asset list (her licenses, her portfolio, her
network, her side skill). The tradesman owns his route, his van, his
book of customers. The freelancer owns her client list, her templates,
her posting schedule. Nowhere does the frame require a company; it
requires a habit of asking, every week, what got built that survives
the week. If the honest answer is nothing, that is exactly where to
aim next.

There is a caveat worth stamping on the page. Ownership in this sense
is still slow. It compounds on Tuesdays rather than in podium
moments. A person can run it correctly for three correct months and
see almost nothing in the numbers, because the money front-runs the
attention only where the attention is consistent. This is why law 8
later in this part, acting today and fully, is not an optional add-on
but the twin of the frame you just met. A picture held and never
worked is called a daydream; the case where it pays is the one where
the five senses eventually are invited, on schedule, to help.

## A Budget for Attention

How much, in hours? An owner's rule of thumb works the same way a
financial budget does: a named ration, paid on schedule. Start from
ten percent. One waking hour in ten is the minimum an owner commits to
building things that survive the workweek: study, relationship work,
assets, maintenance on anything that compounds. Hourly workers
allocate it after the shift. Parents allocate it in fragments across
naps and commutes, because that is what they have. Business owners
usually discover they have been living on five percent and calling it
a hundred, since running a business and building one's assets are
different labors wearing the same name badge. The figure is not the
point. The point is that a ration exists, it is counted, and it
appears on the calendar as reliably as the invoice does. Wherever the
ten percent lands in your diary, a different life is scheduled to
show up a year from now.

Two inventory checks keep the ration honest. First, capital versus
consumption: a course that ends in a work sample is capital; a course
that ends in a feeling of having bought something was, however
entertaining, spending. Second, survivability: at the end of the
week, name the thing that exists now which did not exist before.
If the answer is always "rested," you replenished; you did not build;
that is legitimate some weeks and worth being deliberate about
always.

## One Name on One Thing

Since the frame announces itself mostly in the future tense, close
with the present. Answer for yourself one question with one object:
what is the one asset you are building this quarter? Write
the name of the thing (the bass guitar, the paper license, the
address list, the course, the savings plan) and the sentence of what
you will do with it this month. A named thing is manageable; a
category called "finances" is wind. Owners plan nouns.

Give the noun a review date as well, a single recurring minute on the
calendar when the month's deposit gets checked, because assets that
are never audited quietly convert back into stubs. That is the whole
mechanical meaning of the word "owner" in this chapter: you inspect
what is yours on a schedule you chose. Whether the inspection finds
growth or neglect, the finding goes into the log either way. Owners
run honest books; renters avoid the landlord, and this landlord is
you.

> **Track it:** This week, every time you choose between spending an
