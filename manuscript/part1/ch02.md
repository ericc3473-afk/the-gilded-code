# Getting Rich Is Not Luck; It Is Law

Watch what happens when a house down the street sells for triple what
your mortgage is. Someone says "lucky them." Someone else says "must be
nice to have parents with money." Then a friend mentions a colleague
who flipped two properties while working full-time, and the room goes
quiet, because that third case refuses both explanations. He had the
same starting place you had. He read the same market you read. He
simply did certain things in a certain order, those things turned out
to have the same effect they have on everybody, and after a few years
his life looks like a different species of life.

Wallace Wattles wrote his second chapter to make exactly that point
uncomfortably precise: getting rich is not a matter of luck, and it is
not a matter of mystery. It is an exact science, like mathematics, and
if a person does things in a certain way, the result arrives. He
believed the rule is as dependable as a lever under a stone. Pull the
lever the prescribed way and the stone moves. Pull it wrong and it
does not, whatever you feel about luck that day.

## Like Arithmetic, Not Like Lightning

The dependability is the radical part, so slow down here. If you have
spent years watching money behave unpredictably, the claim sounds
naive. But check the arithmetic. Some people do reliably get rich; it
is the pattern of what they do that looks random. Some flunked out of
school. Some never read a business book. Some are charming, some are
grating, some happened to sell at the right moment, and some worked
one trade for thirty quiet years. What repeats across the set is not
biography. It is conduct: they want a bigger life in a definite way,
they act on that want daily in the direction of their best judgment,
they deliver real value to real people, and they repeat without
interruption. Wattles claimed that anyone who does those things gets
rich, with near-arithmetic certainty, regardless of circumstance,
because the doing is what the getting runs on.

That is why his book is a science and not a sermon. A science tells
you the input that reliably produces the output. He believed you can
learn the input the same way you learn every other practical thing: by
doing it, badly at first, correcting from results. Which takes us to
the objections, because you already own some of them.

## The Three Objections You Carry

**The environment objection** says where you are controls what you
become. Wattles checked the geography instead. In the same city,
people living side by side, children of the same public schools and
the same wages, end up in wildly different circumstances. The poor
neighborhood produces both its martyrs and its magnates. If
environment were the controlling variable, everyone in it would come
out the same, and nobody does. Your location sets the price of things
and the shape of your options. It does not set your direction.

**The talent objection** says only the gifted escape. Wattles points
at the talent that exists and stays poor. Some of the ablest people
you know are underpaid; some of the least exceptional people you know
are comfortable. If talent were the gatekeeper, the gate would sit
where talent sits. It does not sit there. Genius is certainly
valuable, but the evidence says the getting of wealth depends on how
you use what you have, and mediocre people use what they have in a
certain way and rise past the brilliant who don't. This is good news
wearing work clothes: somewhere between your current skill and the
genius of the rare case there is room for you to act better than you
acted last year.

**The capital objection** says you cannot start without money. Wattles
meets it the hardest way he can: the man with the initial dollar was
once a man with nothing, and he got his first dollar by behaving in a
certain way before he had anything to leverage. Every fortune was
bootstrapped across its first low rung by somebody. Capital accelerates
a person who knows the way. It has never been the ticket to the road.

You will notice these three objections share a design. Each one hands
away your agency in exchange for a lowering of blame. That is the
bargain. You get to feel less responsible for the flat years; you pay
with the assumption that nothing you try will work, which quietly
stops you from trying. Consider what the bargain costs over a decade.
The person who says "not for people like us" has usually accepted the
word of a family habit, a bad manager, or one bad year, and made it a
law of physics. Meanwhile the neighbors keep demonstrating, in the
same street and the same pay brackets, that the physics are different.

One question sits the whole matter down plainly. Which do you find
more believable: that a billion dollar economy pays exact rates for
predictable work, tax rules, leases, consulting, trades, all steady,
except that zero of those rates are reachable from your address, or
that you have never yet run the operating instructions end to end? If
you took the second option even only in spirit, this book will hold
you to it for thirty days.

## What the Certain Way Is Not

Now turn the law toward its characteristic abuse. A century of
imitators have read Wattles' first chapters and concluded that
thinking looks like sitting. Picture the money, feel the gratitude, and
wait for delivery. Wattles says the direct opposite, and he says it in
this very chapter: the certain way includes acting, and acting
decisively, in your current circumstances, today. The thought is half
the law. The other half is what you do with the seven waking hours
after it. Think of the two as a paired rule, like keeping your word and
writing it down.

So the law of this chapter, stated for use: **you can learn the way of
acting and thinking that reliably produces wealth, act on it where you
already are, and expect the result with the patience you'd expect of
arithmetic rather than of lightning.** Nobody needs to be born again
into a richer family, no particular skill is required beyond what
today's work needs, and the starting account balance is whatever it is.

Two weeks is enough time to see method, and it is the honest test. Run
one law at a time, fully, rather than testing each one half-heartedly
in parallel. The most common failure mode of this material is a person
executing a rule halfway for a week, seeing nothing, and then blaming
the rule. You would not fill a prescription for three days, feel no
better, and arrive at court against the pharmacist. Give the law the
duration you'd give any device before condemning it, and log honestly,
including the days you skipped. The log is a measuring instrument; a
bent instrument reports what the bender wants.

The cheapest way to test all this is not philosophical. It is to run
the rest of Part I and then start acting in ways you can watch. Fill a
pipe slowly and the sound still reaches the far end within weeks; the
sound of this law working is the first few quiet numbers in your own
ledger, and Part II is where those numbers appear.

> **Do this now:** Write down the objection you lean on hardest when
> you explain why people like you don't get rich: luck, talent, or
> capital. Now find three moments in your own history that break it,
> moments where doing a thing in the certain way beat odds. Put them
> where you'll reread them.
>
> **Track it:** Every time you catch yourself or someone near you
> saying "lucky" about a real outcome, log the sentence and rewrite it
> as its rule: what was actually done, in what order. Ten of these in
> two weeks and the word "lucky" starts sounding unprofessional to
> you, which is the point.
