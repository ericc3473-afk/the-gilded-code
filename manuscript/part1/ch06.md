# The Gratitude Switch

Of every chapter in Wattles' little book, the gratitude one sounds the
softest and, run correctly, does the most mechanical work. He devotes
a whole chapter to thankfulness in the middle of a get-rich manual,
placed before the chapters on will and action, and his claim is blunt:
a mind full of gratitude reliably arrives; a mind full of complaint
reliably doesn't. It is worth translating why, because the imitators
stripped the mechanism out and left the mood, and a mood is not worth
ninety seconds of your morning.

Start with what a complaining mind is busy doing. It is running a
filter pointed mostly at lack: the raise that hasn't arrived, the
manager who doesn't get it, the years lost, the prices in the
supermarket aisle. The filter is not lying. All of those things are
real. The trouble is what the filter feeds: a person scanning for
lack finds lack more efficiently every day, and if what you were
looking for was "everything that is going nowhere," then more
competence at the scan is bad news. (Chapter 2 said the world answers
the person whose attention is ordered. This chapter is about the
order.) Meanwhile the facts that were technically available, the
returning client, the idea nobody locally has tried, the tool you
already own, sit in the same pile unread. Wattles' word for this second
scan, in his sturdier 1910 phrasing, was that complaint cuts the mind
off from the current of supply. Keep the word or don't. Watch the
filter, because the filter is the mechanism.

## Three Things the Switch Does

First, it protects the picture. The definite picture from chapter 5
rests on a platform of "some things in my present life are already
load-bearing": the skill that will pay for the shop, the clients who
will refer, the car that gets you to the second job. Gratitude is the
practice of noticing on purpose what is already working in the
direction of the picture. Neglect that and the picture competes for
air with a counter-list of everything missing, and the counter-list
usually wins on volume. A daily naming of the working things is the
counterweight. The switch makes the attentional ledger honest.

Second, it closes the comparison door. Resentment of other people's
wealth is not merely rude; it is off-task. Wattles points out that
resentment ties your attention, for free and below market rates, to
somebody else's balance sheet, and he reminds the reader that
everything around you was built on the work of people mostly
unfamiliar to you: the road, the vaccine, the lathe, the wheat. (He
could not resist the word "grace" here; the ingredients of your ordinary
morning were mostly gifts.) The practical consequence isn't a
sentiment. It is that no person gets rich by auditing another
person's luck, so if the purpose is your own progress, the audit is
wasted labor. Gratitude is simply the cleanest habit for releasing
the wasted version.

Third, and this is the one nobody expects, gratitude is futures-facing.
To be thankful for the arriving, usable, not-yet-here portions of
your plan, for the open door before it opens, for the work you're
about to do, is to hold the picture with warmth instead of dread, and
the difference matters mechanically. People move toward what they
expect to find pleasant. A mind that rehearses arrival treats its
own Tuesday work as the road into the welcome; a mind climbing
toward a finish line it distrusts stalls at every opportunity to
doubt. Wattles says thankfulness keeps the mind tuned to expect more,
so to speak. Expecting more is what makes you go looking at the
posted rate, ask about the license, and return the buyer's call.

## Running It, Ninety Seconds at a Time

Here is the daily version, which is all you need. Once a day, in the
log, name three working things. Not generic ones (health, family,
coffee, all invaluable and too safe); name the ones wired to the
picture: the afternoon the buyer answered in two lines, the bank
appointment that finally got made, the shoulders that carried the
second night of the shift. Specific gratitude pays best because it
retunes the filter to the exact channels your plan runs on.

And when lack-talk or resentment finds you, because it will this
week, run the switch. The exact drill takes half a minute: name the
trigger in writing, name the three working things, and set one small
task from the picture. That ending task is the difference between
gratitude as a stall and gratitude as a reboot. In the log, mark the
entry "switch" so weeks later you can see the drill's whole
arithmetic: how often the old filter grabbed the wheel, and how often
the new one kept it.

Then there is the honest caveat, in the spirit of this whole Part:
gratitude is a discipline of attention, and it never substitutes for
attention where the trouble is real. You will not positive-think a
leaky roof. If a lawful complaint exists, run it: write the problem,
fix the problem, log the fix. The switch is for the thoughts that
were never going anywhere, the ones that cost you their full sixty
minutes and returned nothing, and that is nearly all of them.

## What the Switch Is not

Three misuses have given the whole practice a bad name, and this book
refuses all three with you. The switch is not a gag. It exists so
that honest complaint, the rare and useful kind, gets loud by
comparison; the habitual kind drowns out the signal the way static
drowns a weather report. It is not an instruction to enjoy bad
circumstances. A bad lease, an abusive boss, a medical bill with no
good options: those get the full treatment this book offers, which is
lawful complaint, then action, then law 10's move to better territory.
And it is not a replacement for doing. Wattles places the gratitude
chapter before the acting ones because gratitude feeds action; anyone
who finishes the day on three thank-yous and no tasks has run the
ritual and skipped the machine.

## In the Hard Weeks

There will be weeks when the picture looks like it was written by an
optimist you no longer employ, because the shift ran short and the
bill came long, and this practice needs its scaled-down form
precisely then. In a hard week, the standing gratitude triple stays
but the size shrinks to what is true even then: the body that
maintained, the caller who paid promptly, the shelf that did not go
empty. Nobody is asking you to be delighted. The question the log
asks is narrower, and you can answer it on any day of any year:
what worked today, however small, in the direction of the life you
intend? A list that keeps that question answerable is not a mood. It
is a handrail, and in the hard weeks it is the difference between a
stopped plan and a slowed one.

The records also agree on this from the other side: people recover
their footing faster when they keep writing them down during the bad
stretches, because the notebook holds evidence that the bad stretch
is a chapter rather than the book. That is what the gratitude pages
are for when the weather turns. Hold the handrail, run the drill,
write the three, and let the plan wait out the storm without
dissolving while you wait.
