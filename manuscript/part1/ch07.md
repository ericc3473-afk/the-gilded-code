# Mind Your Own Mind

Every plan has a weather system around it, and for most people the
weather is other people's opinions. You decide to raise your prices,
and before noon a brother-in-law has made a joke about delusions; a
feed post tells some stranger's sad story about the exact field you
were sniffing at; a friend drops the word "scam" in a group chat and
leaves the group. None of it arrives as an argument. It arrives as
mood, and mood is heavier than it looks, because mood steers the
hours without filing any paperwork.

Wattles writes three chapters about what he calls the use of the will,
and the thing is easy to misread, so start with what he refuses to
mean. The will is not white-knuckle force over the world, and it is
not, as a worrying number of imitators have tried to teach, a tool for
bending other people's minds toward you. He is unusually firm that
using thought on another person's mind, to move them, is misuse on
several grounds at once: it takes your attention off your own
picture, it requires the very scarcity assumptions you just spent
chapter 3 retiring, and it fences with people instead of working
beside material that does not vote. (One of the nicer surprises of his
book is that the metaphysic, held honestly, makes manipulation sound
stupid before it sounds immoral. You cannot order dinner from a
restaurant by staring at the waiter. Yet the persuasion industry
keeps selling the stare.)

## What the Will Is For

What remains, once the misuse is set down, is the most valuable piece
of property you own: your next thought about your own plan. Wattles
calls the will the power to keep thinking what you decided to think,
specifically to hold the picture from chapter 5 in place while the
world coughs up fog. He gives the discipline two working rules. First,
hold thoughts that move you toward the picture and decline to entertain
the ones that move you away from it, and he is unsentimental about
"decline": the thought arrives, you note the weather, and you return
the attention to the item on today's page. Second, never spend the
will trying to be right about the world being closed, dark, or
against you; a mind that rehearses doom gets good at producing
evidence and bad at producing plans.

Translated into today's environment, where the coughing never stops,
this becomes a set of standing house rules, and you get to write
them because the leaky doors are mobile. Say what may interrupt the
plan: the family medical call, the boss's message during work hours,
the neighbor who actually needs a hand. Say what may not: the
recommended video, the comment thread under a stranger, the third
recheck of the market that you priced an hour ago. Wattles' own
drill for this, which is more modern than it sounds, was to stop
feeding on material whose entire income model is your anxiety:
dread-soaked newspapers, in his day, failure fiction, and the
competitive gossip that tells you exactly how badly everyone is
doing. The 2026 edition is a settings page.

## Two Drills That Do the Work

The cheap first drill is the feed diet. For one week, before you
take in anything about money or business, decide in advance whose
voice it is and what it is for: one working source per topic going
into your plan, not a chorus. Adverts, hot takes, doom reports, and
other people's purchases are all instructed to wait outside. notice,
in the log, by Friday, the moment when the plan stops sounding
insane to you for no observable reason. That is the sound of your own
attention coming home, and Wattles is precise that this is the
condition his "certain way" requires to operate.

The second drill handles people more gently than the internet
version, because family is not a notification. When a harmless but
loud sceptic appears, and it is almost always backed by real love
with the volumes misadjusted, you do not fence. You acknowledge
specifically, thank briefly, and return the room to the day's
business; the phrase, said sincerely, is "you might be right about
the past; here is what the plan runs on this month." Doubt about
history is disarmed straight away with facts, and doubt about the
future is not a claim about you; it is a weather report from a
different climate. You log the exchange, note the minutes it cost,
and return the subject where the drill keeps it: on your own mind,
not as a war.

A third drill sets the cadence and closes the loop: the weekly
fifteen. Once a week, on the same day, sit with the log for a quarter
of an hour, next to the chapter-5 picture, and answer three
questions in writing. Did the standing rules hold? What entered the
field, and what would fence it next week? Which single item of the
plan moved? This is the will's maintenance schedule; the other
drills run daily, and fifteen protected minutes a week is what keeps
them from becoming one more thing the noise eventually ate. People
skip this one because it feels administrative, then wonder at month
end why the plan drifted without any visible argument against it.

## Appearances, Not Facts

One caution keeps this chapter honest, because a lazy reader will
glide from here into denial. Holding the picture is not closing your
eyes to the numbers on the envelope. Wattles divides the case
sharply: appearances of lack that are merely habit are to be ignored
on schedule, while facts in the ledger, the overdraft, the deadline,
the illness, are worked as they stand. The discipline is in where
the attention rests the rest of the day, and in refusing to build
permanent conclusions out of temporary readings of the same five
facts you had last month. You will still act on facts; you will
simply refuse to let them stand in for the future, which is not
their jurisdiction, and never was.

The difference is easiest to see on a bad Wednesday. The overdraft
notice arrives at lunch. The appearance-only response spends the
afternoon rehearsing ruin, re-telling the story to a colleague,
checking the balance a ninth time, and deciding, without quite
deciding, that the January plan is over. The facts-first response
takes nine minutes at two o'clock: read what the money actually
did, fix what can be fixed today, book the phone-call that prevents
the repeat, log it, and hand the remainder of the afternoon back to
the day's work. Both days end with the same bank balance. One
ends with a plan still standing, and the cheapest comparison of the
two is not a philosophy debate. It is your own Thursday.

Practically-minded readers will notice what this chapter is actually
purchasing: consistency of hours. Every other law in this part
reruns the picture; tonight's chapter makes each of those reruns
protected from the two debts that keep stealing it, the world's
noise and your own rehearsed doubts. Between the standing rules and
the drills, you build a small, fenced field in which a plan can
survive long enough to compound. Field maintenance is the price of
the fence, and like the ten percent from chapter 4, it is paid on a
schedule, not in moods; the week the maintenance slips is the week
the neighbors' weather moves in.
