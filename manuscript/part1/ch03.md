# Opportunity Is Not Monopolized

There is a sentence people say about every era except their own: "the
opportunities were better then." In the 1880s, the year Barnum's book
came out, people were saying it about the gold rush. In Wattles' time,
factory space and city land were supposedly all claimed. Today the
voice says the internet got crowded in 2013, the property market was
for the last decade only, the trades are saturated. The sentence is a
fixture. It outlives every era it describes, which is the first clue
that it might be describing the speaker rather than the world.

Wattles opens his third chapter by attacking the idea directly,
because the scarcity story is not harmless moping. It stops action. A
person who believes the wealth pool is pre-divided will never look,
never ask, and never move; why bother fishing in a lake you've been
told is empty? So he makes the argument carefully, and it is worth
restating in full because it is one of the most load-bearing paragraphs
in his science.

## The Elastic Supply

His claim: the supply of opportunity is elastic. It stretches for
anyone who moves toward it. New needs keep forming in every economy,
new wants keep surfacing, new combinations keep becoming possible, and
every single one of those is a door with your size on the label. None
of this requires you to be first, or twenty-four, or wealthy. The
world's stock of unmet needs is not a fixed cake being divided; the
cake grows every time somebody wants anything new, and wants are
reproducing faster than ever.

Run his logic against your own neighborhood. In every city there sit,
side by side, the person who has given up calling it a market and the
person who is compiling a list of everyone in the district with an
unfixed problem. One of them believes the ladder has holders at each
rung. The other keeps discovering loose rungs, sometimes the same one
the first person passed over, because momentum and attention find what
resignation cannot. The supply did not differ between the two houses
on Monday morning. Only the reading did.

Also note the direction of the thing. Wattles is careful about what he
does not claim: he does not say every market is open at every moment,
or that a person is wrong to have a harder run in a shrinking town
than her cousin has in a growing one. He says nobody is shut out of
some door somewhere, and the search for that door is part of the work.
About latecomers being locked out he is unmoved: the whole history of
commerce is latecomers. Every dominant firm in any industry spent an
early year being told, in so many words, that its field was already
occupied. The verdict is written by the next person to open a door,
not by the last person to close one.

One refinement matters for the search: doors are personal-sized. The
verdict "no room in that field" is usually true of somebody's door,
and people are careless about whose. The openings that fit a nurse
with twenty years of ward experience have nothing to do with the
openings that fit a carpenter with a van full of tools, and both of
you have been reading the same discouraging pamphlet written about
somebody else. This is why the search is a search, not a referendum
on your era. Sit down and inventory what you already have that was
ever paid for: skills, licenses, lists, tools, hours, temperament.
Every item on that list is a key, and some of those keys fit doors
you stopped checking years ago, because someone in that hallway told
you that hallway was finished.

## Late versus Ready

Distinguish being late from being unready, because they feel similar
from inside and only one of them is real. Late says the window shut
before you arrived, and there is nothing to do. Unready says you have
not accumulated the skill or the quiet to act well yet, and there is
plenty to do. One is a verdict on the calendar. The other is a status
report. Notice how the people who feel late keep using the word about
everyone equally ("there's no point, it's all been done by younger
people") while the people who feel unready always have next actions on
the bench ("I can't price custom jobs yet, so my next ninety days are
learning apps and quoting under supervision"). The first version has
no exits. The second one is just a schedule.

A quick story from the shape of a thousand such cases. A woman in her
forties tells her family the tutoring market is closed; everyone does
it for nothing on the apps, she says, and she waited too long anyway.
Her brother-in-law, a mediocre student and a patient man, spends two
evenings asking around and finds three families who want somebody in
person, on a Wednesday, for a fee double the app rate, and none of
them knew the word "saturated." He is not smarter. He simply did not
carry the closed-door assessment in with him, and he checked a
hallway instead of trusting the pamphlet about it. The gap between
those two evenings was not talent or luck; it was one belief about a
hallway that one person had checked and the other had assumed.

If you carry a favorite closed door, examine whether it is actually
late, or merely unready, or simply guarded by a rumor. Doors that
require "connections" usually require follow-up to an email thread
nobody has sent. Doors that require capital usually require a first
invoice. Doors that require a license usually require a Tuesday
afternoon. Nothing about this is easy, and a chapter that pretended
otherwise would be selling you the scarcity story inverted. The
practical claim is narrower: for any given want, some door is open,
and "no door anywhere" is a feeling, not a survey.

There is one further piece, the quiet part of Wattles' argument: to
the extent that scarcity talk is a habit with physical symptoms, it
tells on you. The complainer's mouth writes the same conclusion over
his own future. The words "everyone I know who tried that failed" are
a map of everyone the speaker happens to know, a sample of maybe a
dozen people out of a million in the same position. It is an
interviewing error, not a market report.

## Your Closed-Door Audit

So the chapter ends in a ledger, where all of Part I ends. During the
coming week, catch and record every "no room for me" thought. For each
one, write downstream: the exact quotation, who or what it blames,
whether the door is actually late or actually unready, and the first
step that costs nothing. The first pass takes minutes. The pattern
across a dozen entries is what you are looking for, and it is almost
always the same shape: three or four rumors masquerading as facts,
holding up one habit of standing still.

Expect the audit to be annoying and short-lived. Most people find the
same three closed doors come back with different wording, which means
your actual list is short, and a list that short can be worked
one item at a time before the season is over. That is the whole
difference between an era that supposedly locked you out and a
Tuesday that did not.
