# The Right to Be Rich

You are allowed to want money. That is where this book starts, and it
starts there because most people quit before they begin. Somewhere
along the way, the wish for money got labelled greedy, and the label
has been quietly vetoing your decisions ever since. You cannot run a
wealth plan well while part of you is apologising for it.

Wallace Wattles opens his book by answering that objection, so let's do
the same here. The wish for money is not a wish for gold bars in a
basement. Money is a claim on other people's work and the world's
stuff: food, medicine, training, travel, tools, time. The wish for more
money is therefore, underneath, a wish for more life. When a person
wants ten thousand dollars, what that really names is what ten thousand
dollars will do. The clerk who wants a bigger salary wants what the
salary changes: the dentist visit, the night class, the holiday he
promised his mother six years ago. Money and the living it buys are
one wish seen from two sides, and the shame attaches to neither.

## It Is a Failure to Live Small

Before the argument, notice that you already agree with it in your
moodier hours. Nobody past twenty honestly wants a small life. Ask any
room of adults who would prefer less health, fewer friendships, duller
work, and a tighter circle of options; watch what people say when
someone else wins a lottery. The desire for reinforcement, for
increase, for a life with more in it, is the default setting of the
species. The shame is a thin coating over that setting, applied by
other people's mouths, and the coating cracks the moment you look at
your own private arithmetic, which is why the guilt feels so false
when you inspect it.

Wattles opens with a claim in his most direct voice, and you should
hear it plainly, because a respectable century has trained you not to.
He says the person who lives and dies poor, in a world that pays
abundantly for useful work, has failed at a practical task, the same
way a farmer who plants nothing in fertile soil has failed. Not wicked.
Failed. The distinction matters, because we forgive with pity what
ought to have been corrected with a plan, and both coatings, the moral
stain and the pity, keep you from taking the subject seriously enough
to learn it. His case, in today's language, runs like this.

You arrive with three kinds of appetite to feed: body, mind, and
heart. The body wants food, rest, warmth, and competent care when it
breaks. The mind wants books, tools, travel, and time that is not
swallowed by exhaustion. The heart wants friends and family, and those
relationships cost attendance: visits, gifts, help offered when help
is expensive. Every item on the list is priced, and every item has a
version you cannot afford. A person can develop only so far on the
crumbs his wallet allows. Wanting wealth in order to become what you
could become is therefore not the lowest motive on the shelf. It is
the load the finer motives ride on.

Your wanting is also not only about you, which is the point exactly
opposite the greedy one. The nurse who never negotiates her salary has
no slack for her mother's care. The tradesman who never buys the good
band-saw remains the cheapest tool on his own site. Slack in a family
has to be earned by someone first, and the someone is usually you.
Households rarely fall behind because one member wanted too much. They
fall behind because everyone silently agreed to want too little.

Notice what Wattles argues against, because it is still the air you
breathe. Generations of respectable writing taught ordinary people
that poverty is noble, that contentment with little is the highest
virtue, and that the rich are vaguely crooked. He refused all of it.
Contentment that follows a full effort is a philosophy. Contentment
used as a substitute for effort is sedation. One of them is chosen
from strength; the other protects you from having to try. And try is
the verb that matters, because without it nothing else in this book
runs.

## The Engine That Has to Work

There is one more piece, and it is the hinge the whole book turns on.
You do your best work as the free version of yourself, the one nobody
is gnawing at from inside. A person worried about next month's rent
gives jumpy service, makes safe choices, and brings a tired brain to
everything. Even employers who love to wrangle over pay would rather
rent calm competence at full price than borrow jittery compliance
cheap. Your future self is a productive asset, and every productive
asset needs working capital. If your best is trapped behind the money
problem, then solving the money problem is maintenance, not
self-indulgence. Wattles goes as far as calling it an obligation, on
the grounds that everyone your best would have served is being let
down while you stay stuck. Keep the word or drop it; the claim is
hard to argue with.

A size note, so nobody hears a promise that is not here. This law says
nothing about yachts. It says the size of the life you get is the size
of the life you actually intend, and most people have been intending
small for so long that the ceiling sits inches above their heads. The
proof is personal. You already know the version of yourself that
works, creates, and shows up; you have been that person for stretches
before. The cost of never intending big is that that person stays
rare. The reward of intending big is that she becomes your default.
Whatever number makes you flinch in the coming chapters, note it in
your log. The flinch is data. It marks the exact spot where the old
ceiling sits.

One caution, because a century of imitators has bolted nonsense onto
this law: none of it justifies dishonest getting. This book keeps
saying so from here to the end. The getting must be honest to work,
which is not a moral decoration but a mechanical requirement you will
meet in full in law 21.

## Where the Wanting Went

Now audit yourself. Most people have automated their smallness so
thoroughly that they cannot find the wanting anymore. You can locate
it in the deferments: the postponed dentist visit, the course
considered for five years, the trip deferred since the raise that
never turned into savings. Every "when I have the money" is a wanting
that has a name. The list runs long, and every item on it is exactly
where this book begins.

Vagueness is where wanting goes to die. "Get my teeth fixed" never
happens; "crown on the cracked molar, $420, appointment Tuesday"
happens. Twenty-two laws will be working on particulars, so make the
particulars. Notice, too, how the taboo makes you hide even the list
from yourself. The deferments sit in a drawer you never open, because
opening it feels like complaining about your own life. Open it once
today. A wanting named is a want you can work on; a wanting hidden is
a want that quietly runs you.
