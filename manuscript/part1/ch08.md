# Do All That Can Be Done Today

Imagine two readers finishing the last chapter. One has a beautiful
picture, a full log, a tidy week of standing rules; by February she
has nothing new to show for it, because the whole apparatus has been
running as a thinking hobby. The other started with a messier picture
and did one unglamorous act the same evening, then another, and
February finds her with a waiting list, a course finished, and a
strange new problem: the plan has begun to make requests of her.
Pictures do not pay. Neither do drills. The paying happens in the
acts between, and Wattles, who has been accused by a century of
imitators of preaching thought-only success, spends his longest
chapters making sure you never, for one day, confuse the picture with
the work.

His position is almost rude in its simplicity, and worth slowing
down for because it dismantles several favorite excuses at once. You
cannot act where you are not, so the planning that happens "once I
move, once I'm qualified, once the market turns" is not action at
all; it is the picture wearing a costume of action. You cannot act
where you have been, so the replay of old failures is not practice;
it is theater with no audience. You can act only where you are:
this desk, this shift, this flat, this town, today's minutes and
tools, with the picture giving the direction and the day giving the
ingredients. Anything else is postponement with a library card.

## The Capacity of the Day

Wattles' standard for a day is quietly severe, and this is the heart
of the law: do, each day, all that can be done that day. Note the
word *can*. Not all that could be done by a fictional version of you
with superior energy and no children; not everything on a list
written in optimism at 9 p.m. All that can actually be accomplished,
by the actual person, on the actual Tuesday: the amount that fits
the body you brought to work and the hours the day actually holds.
Most failing plans are not under-ambitious; they are misloaded,
built on a Tuesday that only exists in daydreams, and the first bad
morning tips them over. The "can" standard is merciful precisely
because it is real: it asks for the whole day you have, and no other.

The companion piece is what this day is *for*. Every action either
carries you toward the picture or away from it, and the day's work
should therefore include, every day, some act the picture would
recognize as its own: the flyer designed, the first client asked,
the application posted, the meeting booked, the application for the
license submitted. Smallness is no excuse. Wattles is exact here
that you must hold the picture while acting, but you must act, and
the act can be tiny. A tiny act done today in the right direction
outruns a large act planned for Thursday evening that Thursday
evening will not contain; the calendar can always be argued with
from now until next winter.

One trick keeps the two halves of this law welded in practice.
When you plan the day, the night before or over first coffee, there
are only ever two columns to fill, and the discipline is refusing
to add a third: what today *contains* (the shifts, the school run,
the appointment), and what today *builds* (the acts for the
picture, per chapter 4's ten percent). The third column, the one
where hope stores its plans for a mythical future you with free
evenings and spare energy, is banned by name. Everything in it
either gets scheduled into column two or admitted honestly to be a
wish, which then gets logged as a wish. The point is not to shame
the wishes; it is to stop them masquerading as plans, since the
masquerade is precisely how four years can pass with the picture
untouched.

## Efficiency Is a Property of Single Acts

Wattles' second chapter on action makes the point that decides
whether all of this compounds or merely churns: each single act is
itself a success or a failure, and the number of efficient acts, not
the number of hours, is what adds up. The efficient act is the one
you would be willing to have a professional watch, start to finish,
and sign: the call actually made rather than drafted in
imagination, the two paragraphs that end with a period, the tool
actually put away in its place with the next user able to find it.
A rushed act, a half-written message, a scattered afternoon where
four things are touched and nothing is completed does not count as
four movements toward the picture; it counts as noise with good
intentions. Wattles' test for an act is a simple one: whether that
single act, taken alone, is a success. Two consequences follow, and they are the
difference between people who run this law and people who merely
hope. First, fewer acts, finished. The morning that contains three
completed acts beats the day that touches nine. If your current
workday produces many touches and few finishes, the law's
instruction is not "try harder"; it is "close the booth": pick
stop-points where an act can actually be realized, and defend them
the way chapter 7 defended your attention. Second, no haste.
Wattles, who loved a quiet metaphor, points out that hurrying is
self-announced distrust of the picture, and urgency done badly
spills what it is carrying. You can move with great speed and no
hurry: speed is the rate of your work, and hurry is the fear in
it. The first is an engine; the second is a kind of leaking
bucket.

## What Waiting Actually Is

One more field to clear, because Part I ends here and the field is
the favorite parking spot of every other law: "I'm waiting until I
know more / until the money's steadier / until after the holidays."
Some waiting is real, as any nurse or builder can tell you, and the
test is whether the waiting has a job. Real waiting has a date, a
condition, and what you are doing each day to be ready when the
condition arrives; it is rowing with a schedule, and it shows up in
the log. Fake waiting has none of those; it is a story for the
non-doing, run on infinite repeat. When this chapter is done, you
will know which kind your waiting is, because only one of them ever
produced a line in a log.

Part I ends here, and the shape of it should be visible from the
top: you are permitted to want (1); what you want goes through
learnable rules (2) rather than closed doors (3); you feed the rules
with an owner's attention (4), a definite picture (5), a gratitude
filter (6) and a fenced mind (7); and the whole circulation ends, as
the day ends, in the doing (8). Part II takes the shape and installs
it on work, invoices, tools, hours, and people, which is where
money, as opposed to intention, actually lives.
