# See It Before You Have It

Ask a person flat broke what they want and they say "money." Ask them
how much life the money is for and the answer surprises nobody, which
is the same thing, and then the wanting has no shape, no price, and no
next step. Vagueness is doing all of the work here, and it is doing it
in the dark. The reason so many people stay stuck is not that they
want too little. It is that they want *exactly this much and this
long*, approximately, someday, and the world, under any reading of
Wattles you accept, has no purchase order to fill.

Wattles calls the answer to that a definite picture, "the certain way"
of thinking, and it is the closest thing in his whole book to a
recipe. It has steps. You form the clearest, most generous, most
specific image you can of the life you intend: not "rich," but the
street, the workweek, the patients or customers or crew, the exact
money on some exact date. You hold that image with the same steadiness
you'd hold a photograph. You back it with a faith that talks in the
future perfect tense, "it is coming, and it is mine to make." Then you
hand the picture over to your workweek and let it dictate Tuesday.

Before you give that the eye-roll it has earned, distinguish the two
versions that have traveled under this banner for a century. The
counterfeit version says the image is an order form and the world is
the courier, and the mathematics of it fails on contact because
nothing ships. The Wattles version, which is the only one in his
actual book, says the image is a specification: it tells you what
market to study, what calls to make, what skills to pick up this
year, what to say no to at dinner, and what counts as on-track when
you review your week. A specification, held in high definition,
generates all of that. A wish generates nothing, which is why the
people who report the technique failed can rarely describe what their
picture was about.

Notice, too, the protective function of the fog. A vague want can
never be disappointed, and that is its appeal. "More money someday"
cannot miss; the exact shop, booked month, and trained employee can.
People protect the self from failing to obtain an exact thing by
declining to name it, and then spend years quietly surprised that
nothing exact arrived. The fog is a thinking shortcut, yes, but it is
also an insurance policy against disappointment whose premium gets
paid in outcomes.

## Where Vague Goes Wrong

The most concrete evidence available on this is your own phone
history. Whatever you have wanted with precision, you have gotten or
come close to: the exact exam passed, the exact raise once asked for
by name, the exact tiling job you and your spouse argued into
existence, the exact trips taken before the child outgrew the
magic-window years. Vagueness arrived in the same years; that is
where the record goes dark. The world did not stop responding to you.
It stopped responding to fog.

So Part I's centre of gravity, the piece that either makes the rest
of the machine plausible or leaves it inert, is the writing exercise
this chapter exists for. Wattles' instruction is that the picture be
detailed and held daily, and the instruction has a reason that
survives translation: a person who reviews a concrete picture every
day is recalibrating her own attention to the very signals that lead
there. The contact who knows the buyer, the book chapter that was
sitting unopened, the trade angle skipped last year because it "wasn't
her thing": all of these get caught by the net the picture casts.
Direction, not magic. People with detailed five-year pictures spend
more of their hours on things that lead to five-year outcomes,
because the doing follows a held, distinct idea rather than a slogan.

## Writing the Picture

The definition of done for this law, and this is the first exercise
in the book with real weight: one page, present tense, written,
specific. "I work four days a week in my own shop; the sum in the
business account is this figure; my mother's trip is booked for the
signing month; my calendar shows these three half-days with my kids."
Senses in it. Numbers in it. The purpose is visible in every clause.

Three rules keep the document honest.

1. Anything you would be embarrassed to read aloud is probably a
   borrowed picture; anchoring your plan to someone else's life makes
   it impossible to maintain the attention part. Delete the borrowed
   clauses.
2. Every noun gets a date. The years play the role of the arithmetic
   in chapter 2, and a picture with no dates is a photo of a wall.
3. Attachment and handoff. The picture stays in your head like a
   drawing in the notebook, not like a pet. The next chapter hands it
   to the log once a day and then closes the notebook.

A worked example makes the rules concrete. "I want a better pastry
side of the business" is fog; no market, no calls, nothing to rehearse
at dinner. "By December next year, the counter case holds eight
regimented lines; weekend wholesale runs to four cafés at $2.10 per
piece; the account holds one month of ingredients; my Thursday
afternoon holds the 5 to 7 class slot; Saturday mornings I bake with
my daughter before open" is a picture; and the difference is not
aesthetic. Every clause of the second version hands you a to-do, a
price to learn, a person to call, or a room to negotiate. The fog
version is off by one trick: it describes a feeling and calls that a
plan.

Nobody writes this page in one sitting, and Wattles never pretended
otherwise. Write the third honest draft, not the first inspired one.
First drafts come out as borrowed lives, all yachts and airports;
second drafts apologize and go small; third drafts have your
signature, your family, your town, your Tuesday, your practical
dentist appointment. The odd thing about a real picture is how small
it looks next to the fog, which is exactly how you know it is real.
Fog costs nothing to inflate.

You will not be surprised to learn where this is headed, since every
chapter hands its weight to the log. The picture is going to sit at
the front of your book for the next twenty-nine days, between laws,
and you are going to do one thing with it, briefly: open it, read the
relevant piece before the day's work, and put the notebook away. The
weeks do the rest. This is the whole engine you have heard about at
parties, except the version that actually runs includes the day's
working hours in its circuit, which the parties rarely mention.

One practical placement note, because forgetting is the main
failure mode here. Keep the picture physically at the front of the
notebook where the log lives, not in a phone app six screens from the
messaging and the weather. The pairing matters: page, then day, then
close. People who store the picture where their notifications track
them find that the world's loudest voices outbid a still page a
hundred times a day. Paper outlasts the phone at exactly this one
job, and it costs a dollar.

> **Do this now:** Write the one-page present-tense picture. Full
> sentences, real numbers, at least three senses, no borrowed clauses.
> When the page is done, reread it once and then put it at the very
> front of your log where sentences from every future chapter will
> touch it.
>
> **Track it:** In the log's margin, two symbols. One mark for each
> day you did the day's version of the picture's work; one mark for
> days it stayed closed in the notebook. By month's end the marks
> themselves will tell you what Part II is really about.
